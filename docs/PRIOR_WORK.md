# Prior work and positioning review

Project: a two stage stochastic water allocation model for the Egyptian Nile Delta, with a priced staple food guarantee and district water values.

Purpose of this document: to map what already exists before any paper is written, to name the closest neighbours fairly, and to fix what this project may honestly claim and what it must never claim. Every work cited below was verified from a retrieved public source by one of three researchers, then tested by a skeptic whose job was to kill the project novelty claims. Where a work could not be fully verified, it was dropped, and the drops are listed at the end.

## How to read this map

The field splits into traditions that each do one part of what this project attempts, and none of them does all of it. Stochastic method lives at basin and reservoir scale. Cropping optimization lives at national scale and is almost all deterministic. Water values for Egypt exist, but at national scale or by farm survey, not as district duals under inflow scenarios. Food security floors exist, but their cost is priced at one fixed level at most, and never as a curve at Delta scale. Egyptian institutions allocate water by simulation, monitoring and area caps, not by prices. The sections below give the evidence, tradition by tradition.

## Section A. The field map

### A1. Basin and Eastern Nile hydro economic models

Whittington, D., Wu, X. and Sadoff, C. (2005). Water resources management in the Nile basin: The economic value of cooperation. Water Policy, 7 (3). The Nile Economic Optimization Model maximizes total benefits from irrigated agriculture and hydropower across the whole basin and values cooperation at 7 to 11 billion US dollars a year in direct gross benefits. Deterministic, country level nodes, no within Egypt allocation, no food floor. Link: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2336813

Jeuland, M., Wu, X. and Whittington, D. (2017). Infrastructure development and the economics of cooperation in the Eastern Nile. Water International, 42 (2), pages 121 to 141. The same model family applied to the Eastern Nile under different cooperation regimes and hydrology scenarios, centred on the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam. Annual and deterministic; the authors state it does not manage multi year hydrological sequences. Link: https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/rwinxx/v42y2017i2p121-141.html

Goor, Q., Halleux, C., Mohamed, Y. and Tilmant, A. (2010). Optimal operation of a multipurpose multireservoir system in the Eastern Nile River Basin. Hydrology and Earth System Sciences, 14, pages 1895 to 1908. True stochastic programming, solved by Stochastic Dual Dynamic Programming, for reservoir operation, hydropower and aggregate irrigated agriculture across the Eastern Nile. Inflow uncertainty is the stochastic element. Egypt appears as the downstream user of the High Aswan Dam, not as Delta districts. No crop by crop land allocation, no food guarantee, no district water price. Link: https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/0b75/eec0c3943a2a8f048071f6a725220d64365f.pdf

Arjoon, D., Mohamed, Y., Goor, Q. and Tilmant, A. (2014). Hydro economic risk assessment in the eastern Nile River basin. Water Resources and Economics, 8, pages 16 to 31. A stochastic hydro economic model of Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt that maximizes expected net returns from irrigation and hydropower and assesses the risk of those returns, with the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam as the central question. No within Egypt reallocation and no food security constraint. Link: http://www.oalib.com/references/21828239

Kahsay, T. N., Arjoon, D., Kuik, O., Brouwer, R., Tilmant, A. and van der Zaag, P. (2019). A hybrid partial and general equilibrium modeling approach to assess the hydro economic impacts of large dams: The case of the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam in the Eastern Nile River basin. Environmental Modelling and Software, 117, pages 76 to 88. The stochastic basin allocation model feeds a general equilibrium model of the wider economy. The engineering layer holds only a small number of irrigation sites per country. No Delta governorates, no farm margin objective, no water price a Delta planner could use. Link: https://research.vu.nl/ws/files/118478630/A_hybrid_partial_and_general_equilibrium_modeling_approach_to_assess_the_hydroeconomic_impacts_of_large_dams.pdf

Basheer, M., Nechifor, V., Calzadilla, A., Siddig, K., Etichia, M., Whittington, D., Hulme, M. and Harou, J. J. (2021). Collaborative management of the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam increases economic benefits and resilience. Nature Communications, 12, 5622. A monthly river system model coupled both ways with a computable general equilibrium model of Egypt, run on stochastic hydrologic sequences. Coordinated dam operation cut Egypt irrigation deficits and raised Egypt gross domestic product in most scenarios. Egypt is one macro economy here: no crop allocation, no food floor, no district water prices. This is the strongest verified treatment of Nile inflow risk feeding an Egyptian economic model. Link: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-021-25877-w?fromPaywallRec=false

Boehlert, B., Strzepek, K. and Robinson, S. (2017). Analyzing the economy wide impacts on Egypt of alternative GERD filling policies. Chapter in The Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam and the Nile Basin, Routledge. A Nile water system model linked to an economy wide model of Egypt for dam filling policy. National scale only. Links: https://cs3.mit.edu/news-media/news-outreach/grand-ethiopian-renaissance-dam-and-nile-basin-implications-transboundary and https://www.routledge.com/The-Grand-Ethiopian-Renaissance-Dam-and-the-Nile-Basin-Implications-for/Yihdego-Rieu-Clarke-Cascao/p/book/9781138064898

Basheer, M., Wheeler, K. G., Ribbe, L., Majdalawi, M., Abdo, G. and Zagona, E. (2018). Quantifying and evaluating the impacts of cooperation in transboundary river basins on the Water Energy Food nexus: The Blue Nile Basin. Science of the Total Environment, 630, pages 1309 to 1323. A daily simulation built with RiverWare and crop water models over 120 cooperation and infrastructure scenarios. It does not cover Egypt or the Delta, and it simulates rather than optimizes. Included because it shows the simulation tradition that dominates basin work. Link: https://inis.iaea.org/records/6ypbd-qkp09

Nile Basin Initiative (2009 to 2012). Nile Basin Decision Support System. Built by DHI on MIKE software, funded by the World Bank, deployed in all basin countries by September 2012. Tools include MIKE HYDRO Basin, MIKE HYDRO River, MIKE SHE and rainfall runoff models, plus scenario management, cost benefit analysis and multi criteria analysis. It is a simulation and multi criteria platform. It prices neither water nor any food floor. Links: https://nilebasin.org/sites/default/files/2023-09/NBDSS-Technical_Brochure.pdf and https://nbdss.nilebasin.org/support/solutions/articles/4000041022-what-are-the-available-modeling-tools-in-the-dss-

### A2. Egypt sector models and the water pricing tradition

Lofgren, H. (1996). Cost of managing with less: cutting water subsidies and supplies in Egypt's agriculture. IFPRI TMD Discussion Paper 7. A mathematical programming sector model of Egyptian agriculture, the Agricultural Sector Model of Egypt lineage. A 15 percent cut in agricultural water supply raised farmer incomes with moderate consumer harm; a 30 percent cut caused disproportionately larger harm. Water charges were tested as policy. National scale, deterministic, no food floor. Links: https://ideas.repec.org/p/ags/iffp23/97766.html and http://www.ifpri.org/publication/cost-managing-less

He, L., Tyner, W. E., Doukkali, R. and Siam, G. (2006). Policy options to improve water allocation efficiency: Analysis on Egypt and Morocco. Water International, 31 (3), pages 320 to 337. Earlier version: Selected Paper, American Agricultural Economics Association Annual Meeting, 2005. The Egypt sector model, calibrated with Positive Mathematical Programming, derives shadow prices for irrigation water by solving the model at different water quantities: 0.036 Egyptian pounds per cubic metre at a 5 percent reduction in water capacity and 0.083 at a 10 percent reduction, in the money of the time. Taxes on fertilizer, energy and water intensive crop output are tested as stand ins for a water price. National, deterministic, no food floor priced. Link: https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/nanna/record/19467/files/sp05he02.pdf?withWatermark=0&withMetadata=0&registerDownload=1&version=1

McCarl, B. A., Musumba, M., Smith, J. B., Kirshen, P., Jones, D., El Ganzori, M., Ali, M. A., Kotb, T., El Shinnawy, I., El Agizy, I., Bayoumi, M. and Hynninen, K. (2015). Climate change vulnerability and adaptation strategies in Egypt's agricultural sector. Mitigation and Adaptation Strategies for Global Change, 20, pages 1097 to 1109. The Agricultural Sector Model of Egypt run as a price endogenous model of crop and livestock production and water flows for 2030 to 2060 scenarios. Its reference list documents the institutional history of the model inside Egyptian and donor practice, including a 1999 review prepared for USAID Egypt and a 2013 listing by the Ministry of Water Resources and Irrigation. National scale, no district water prices, no food floor. Link: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11027-013-9520-9

Gohar, A. A. and Ward, F. A. (2010). Gains from expanded irrigation water trading in Egypt: An integrated basin approach. Ecological Economics, 69, pages 2535 to 2548. A dynamic nonlinear basin model that maximizes total agricultural income in Egypt along the Nile, subject to hydrologic, environmental, food security and institutional constraints, and compares trading rules from restricted to expanded. Farm income is reported by region, season and policy against a base of about 7.92 billion US dollars of national farm income, with staple crop income held unchanged by the constraints, so the cost of the food security constraints is visible in the policy comparison. Later work cites gains of up to about 28 percent in national farm income from more efficient allocation. No stochastic inflow, no Delta only district set, and the food constraints are held, not swept and priced level by level. Link: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/water/articles/10.3389/frwa.2023.1138869/xml

Gohar, A. A. and Ward, F. A. (2013). Mitigating impacts of water shortage on Egyptian agriculture: a catchment scale analysis. Water Policy, 15 (5), pages 738 to 760. Egypt wide water supply reductions of 10 and 20 percent, with national farm income losses reported with and without limited water trading; trading cuts the losses by about a third. Published proof that dry year stress testing of Egyptian allocations against a baseline is established practice. Link: https://iwaponline.com/wp/issue/15/5

Gohar, A. A., Cashman, A. and El bardisy, H. A. E. H. (2021). Modeling the impacts of water land allocation alternatives on food security and agricultural livelihoods in Egypt: Welfare analysis approach. Environmental Development, 39, 100650. The fullest Egypt allocation model found. Built in GAMS, with 10 river gauge nodes, 13 diversion, consumption and return nodes, 2 reservoirs, 22 crops, 3 irrigation seasons, a 10 year span and 4 optimization policies combining water optimization and land optimization on or off. It tracks land in production, water flows, net agricultural benefits, consumer surplus, producer surplus and total welfare, and it is hydrologically balanced to actual gauge flows for a base year. Inflow is set to the historical average rather than drawn as scenarios. Food security is an outcome tracked, not a floor swept and priced, and no district level table of water duals is published with it. Model documentation: https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/tfh92c5jtz/1

Al Riffai, P., Breisinger, C., Mondal, M. H. A., Ringler, C., Wiebelt, M. and Zhu, T. (2017). Linking the economics of water, energy, and food: A nexus modeling approach. IFPRI Egypt Strategy Support Program, Egypt SSP Working Paper 04. A methodology paper for a linked modelling suite. Its Water Management Optimization Model for the Nile Valley maximizes economic benefits from crop and hydropower production through optimal regulation and allocation of water over time, space and crop activities, with each irrigated area treated as a representative farm, and it handles the stochastic nature of weather and river flows implicitly through a long hydrological record. It publishes no solved Delta district result, no food guarantee price and no district water duals. Treat it as framing, not as a competing result. Link: https://EconPapers.repec.org/paper/fprmenawp/4.htm

### A3. Stochastic programming on Egyptian cropping

Rosa, C. H. and Yates, D. (1994). Addressing the Issue of Uncertainty Within the Egyptian Agricultural Sector. IIASA Working Paper WP 94 097. The authors use the static Egyptian Agricultural Sector Model with stochastic programming methodology, and investigate how Egyptian cropping patterns might change in response to changing annual water availability. Regional production decisions are framed as first stage decisions and national import and export decisions as second stage decisions. This single citation ends any claim that stochastic programming is new to Egyptian cropping. Link: https://core.ac.uk/outputs/33895584/

El Shazly, F. A., Mansour, M. E. E., Ahmed, M. A. and Shehata, E. A. (2009). Egyptian Cropping Pattern Under Risk and Varying Domestic and International Circumstances. Conference Towards Development of New Policies to Promote the Agricultural Sector in Egypt, Faculty of Economics and Political Science, Cairo University, with FAO and partner institutes, Volume 1, Number 1, pages 1 to 48. MPRA Paper 42634. One of its three risk models is explicitly a two stage stochastic programming model, with monthly irrigation water constraints across the year, and food security among its stated purposes. National cropping scale, not Delta governorates, and no swept and priced staple floor or district water duals. Link: https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/42634/

Huang, G. H. and Loucks, D. P. (2000). An inexact two stage stochastic programming model for water resources management under uncertainty. Civil Engineering and Environmental Systems, 17 (2), pages 95 to 118. Not Egypt work. Cited because it anchors the general methods record: two stage stochastic programming for water resources, with first stage targets and second stage recourse under uncertain inflows, has been textbook material for over twenty five years. Link: https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/2137887

### A4. Deterministic cropping and irrigation optimization for Egypt and the Delta

Ali, H. M. and Mahmoud, M. R. (1999). Determining optimal allocation and crop pattern in Egypt by the use of linear programming. Proceedings of the Seventh Nile 2002 International Conference, Cairo, and Hassan Ismail Award Paper, 17th ICID Congress, Granada. A linear program minimizing irrigation water use by choice of crop areas, in seven cases that vary how far areas may move from the actual 1993 pattern, with reported savings of 0.974 to 8.185 billion cubic metres. National scale in three regions only. It minimized water rather than maximizing margin, carried no income against food trade off inside one run, and reported no water prices. Link: https://icid.org/hassan_1999.pdf

Dawoud, S. D. Z. (2014). Economic optimal allocation of irrigation water in Egypt. Journal of Development and Agricultural Economics, 6 (12), pages 472 to 480. Two separate linear programs, one maximizing net return and one minimizing irrigation water, on official data for 2009 to 2011. Maximizing net return raised returns by 3.56 percent against the actual pattern with 3.24 percent water saving; minimizing water saved 11.05 percent of water and cut net returns by 10.20 percent. National, deterministic, no food floor, no water duals published. Link: https://academicjournals.org/journal/JDAE/article-full-text/C9477E548600

Mohamed, A. A. A., Salem, M. H., Moursi, B. M. and Medaney, M. A. (2013). Economic return of alternative cropping pattern in the different agro ecological zones of Egypt. Egyptian Journal of Agricultural Research, 91 (3), pages 1131 to 1158, with a companion paper in 91 (2), pages 745 to 772, maximizing total net return per unit of water. Ain Shams University and Agricultural Research Center authors. Zone level linear programs against the actual pattern: in zone 1 the alternative cut water need by about 1.2 billion cubic metres and added about 1.1 billion Egyptian pounds of net income. Five agro ecological zones, not Delta governorates, no scenarios, no food guarantee, no water duals. Link: https://ejar.journals.ekb.eg/article_167088.html

Hamada, Y. M. (2011). Water Resources Reallocation in Upper and Middle Egypt. European Water, 33, pages 33 to 44. A linear program over five governorates that reallocates while respecting food security, and states the income consequence directly: under the optimal pattern, income fell by 9.653 percent while water use fell 3.308 percent and sugar production rose 14.689 percent. It also tabulates the optimal sugar cropping pattern against the existing pattern, governorate by governorate. Upper and Middle Egypt, not the Delta. Link: http://ewra.net/ew/pdf/EW_2011_33_04.pdf

Ramadan, E. M., Abdelwahab, H. F., Vranayova, Z., Zelenakova, M. and Negm, A. M. (2021). Optimization based proposed solution for water shortage problems: A case study in the Ismailia Canal, East Nile Delta, Egypt. Water, 13, 2481. A linear model with mass balance constraints that measures shortage at demand nodes on the Ismailia Canal and redistributes water from the canal, groundwater and reuse by node preference, against a measured deficit of 789.81 million cubic metres a year. Crops enter only as aggregate agricultural demand. The decision served is canal lining and supply choice, not cropping. Link: https://www.MDPI.com/2073-4441/13/18/2481

Abdelwahab, H. F., Negm, A. M., Ramadan, E. M., Kuriqi, A. and Abd Elaty, I. (2024). Mitigating water shortages and enhancing food security through crop optimization: Insights from the Eastern Nile Delta. Acta Scientiarum Polonorum, Formatio Circumiectus, 23 (4), pages 61 to 76. A deterministic linear program for the Eastern Delta on the Ismailia Canal that cuts crops labelled non strategic and raises crops labelled strategic, including wheat, cotton and maize. Food security is handled by crop labels, not by a minimum production constraint whose cost is priced. Link: https://acta.urk.edu.pl/Mitigating-Water-Shortages-and-Enhancing-Food-Security-through-Crop-Optimization,193864,0,1.html

Sardo, M., Chiarelli, D. D., Ceragioli, F. and Rulli, M. C. (2024). Optimized crop distributions in Egypt increase crop productivity and nutritional standards, reducing the irrigation water requirement. Science of the Total Environment, 951, 175202. A national linear optimization that maximizes green water productivity subject to meeting the dietary requirements of the EAT Lancet diet, reporting 95 percent average satisfaction of healthy food demand, 8 percent lower local freshwater consumption and 90 percent higher crop profitability. The food constraint is a full diet basket, not a staple tonnage guarantee swept and priced, and there are no Delta districts, inflow scenarios or water prices. Link: https://www.mindat.org/reference.php?id=17552493

### A5. Shadow prices and values of water for Egypt

Yates, D. N. and Strzepek, K. M. (1998). An assessment of integrated climate change impacts on the agricultural economy of Egypt. Climatic Change, 38, pages 261 to 287. A quadratic programming sector model that defines the shadow price of water correctly, as the change in the objective from one more unit of the resource, and reports it across climate scenarios: zero in 1990, when water was in surplus relative to land, and positive by 2060. National scale with agro climatic zones, not governorate duals in money per cubic metre. Link: https://research.fit.edu/media/site-specific/researchfitedu/coast-climate-adaptation-library/africa/egypt--libya/Yates--Strezepek.-1998.-Egypt-Agricultural-Economics--CC.pdf

Fuglie, K., Dhehibi, B., El Shahat, A. A. I. and Aw Hassan, A. (2021). Water, policy, and productivity in Egyptian agriculture. American Journal of Agricultural Economics, 103 (4), pages 1378 to 1397. An optimal resource allocation model used to derive shadow values of water for Egypt from 1961 to 2016. The implicit value of water rose from 190 Egyptian pounds per 1000 cubic metres in the 1960s to 722 per 1000 cubic metres in 2001 to 2016, in constant 2005 pounds, roughly 0.722 pounds per cubic metre. The central warning matters for this project: average water productivity, crop per drop, likely overstates the marginal value of water significantly. Backward looking national accounting, not a forward district allocation tool. Link: https://ideas.repec.Org/a/wly/ajagec/v103y2021i4p1378-1397.html

Saleh, E. M. (2018). The economic value and cost recovery of water in the Egyptian irrigated agriculture. Egyptian Journal of Agricultural Research, 96 (2), pages 787 to 813. Residual method values of water by crop from a survey of 80 farmers in Al Satamony Village, Belkas District, Dakahlia Governorate, in the Delta, with rice and clover ranked first, then sugar beet, wheat and maize. District level water values inside a Delta governorate therefore exist, but a residual per crop is not a system dual: it does not say what one more cubic metre is worth after land is reallocated across crops under a water cap. Link: https://ejar.journals.ekb.eg/?_action=export&rf=ris&rc=136213

Fitch, J. B., Khedr, H. A. and Whittington, D. (1981). The economic efficiency of water use in Egyptian agriculture: Opening round of a debate. AgEcon Search, Occasional Papers 1981, number 064. The historical origin of the residual tradition: values of water from full year crop rotations in the Delta, Middle Egypt and Upper Egypt, with mathematical programming discussed and set aside as data intensive. No optimization and no duals. Link: https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/nanna/record/197445/files/agecon-occpapers-1981-064_1_.pdf?withWatermark=0&withMetadata=0&registerDownload=1&version=1

### A6. Food security floors and income trade offs

Adly, N., Nosier, S., Kassem, N., Mahrous, M. and Salah, R. (2018). Modelling the optimal cropping pattern to 2030 under different climate change scenarios: A study on Egypt. African Journal of Agricultural and Resource Economics, 13 (3), pages 224 to 239. A national linear program over 26 crops, run year by year from 2013 to 2030 under climate scenarios, on a land base of 4,787,468 hectares from the CAPMAS Yearbook 2013, with ministry cost and price data. One constraint requires the wheat area to be at least the area achieving self sufficiency, defined as 50 percent of wheat consumption. With the floor against without it, wheat area grows about 1 percent a year against 0.2 percent, and total net revenue falls by 13.3 percent. That fall is a computed cost of a food floor in an Egypt model. The floor level is fixed, not varied; there is no price curve for the floor and no water price by district; the model is deterministic, with climate scenarios but no inflow scenarios and no probabilities. Link: https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/nanna/record/284987/files/3.-Adly-et-al.pdf?withWatermark=0&withMetadata=0&registerDownload=1&version=1

Bazaraa, M. S. and Bouzaher, A. (1981). A linear goal programming model for developing economies with an illustration from the agricultural sector in Egypt. Management Science, 27 (4), pages 396 to 413. A multi regional linear goal program with goals for employment, foreign exchange and regional demand satisfaction for basic nutrition beside economic goals, with land, labour, water, machinery, fertilizer and capital constraints and explicit crop rotation rules. Goals are targets with penalized deviations, not a hard staple floor with a reported dual price. No stochastic inflow and no Delta water dual. Link: http://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v27y1981i4p396-413.html

El Sayed, L. M. (2012). Determining an optimum cropping pattern for Egypt. Master of Arts thesis, American University in Cairo. Fuzzy goal programming over five goals, on Ministry of Agriculture and FAO data. Without a food security constraint the model drops strategic crops including wheat, broad beans and maize as not optimal, so minimum land allocations for strategic crops were imposed, and the thesis concludes the cost is tolerable, with the winter profit goal missed by only 0.68 percent. Food security enters as minimum crop areas and a production goal, not as a tonnage guarantee swept to trace a price, and there is no district split and no water dual. Link: https://fount.aucegypt.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=3417&context=retro_etds

Abdelkader, A. and Elshorbagy, A. (2021). ACPAR: A framework for linking national water and food security management with global conditions. Advances in Water Resources, 147, 103809. Earlier version: Elshorbagy, A. and Abdelkader, A. (2019), 3rd World Irrigation Forum, Bali. A multi objective framework that generates non dominated national cropping patterns trading off agricultural water demand, food and virtual water imports, import cost and national gross margin, built on historical patterns from 1986 to 2013 and evaluated to 2050 under national and global scenarios. Patterns are filtered by a rule that food self sufficiency should not fall below 85 percent of the historical level. The trade off is shown as a set of patterns, not as the dual price of one guarantee constraint. No Delta district resolution and no inflow probability structure. Links: https://www.icid.org/wif3_bali_2019/wif3_1-1_46-min.pdf and https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11269-024-03758-3

Guevara Polo, A. S., Patiño Gomez, J., Medellin Azuara, J. and Corona Vasquez, B. (2025). Insights from Hydro Economic Modeling for Climate Resilience in the Nazas Aguanaval Watershed in Mexico. Water, 17, 3183. The nearest non Egypt analogue for a floor and a water price moving together: a Positive Mathematical Programming hydro economic model of one irrigated watershed holds observed forage production as a floor in one scenario; as water availability falls from 100 to 55 percent, the water shadow price rises from 16 to 542 US dollars per thousand cubic metres and maximum profit falls from 76 million to 21 million US dollars. The floor is forage tonnage at one fixed level, not a staple guarantee varied level by level, and the prices are for one watershed, not district by district inside a delta. Link: https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/17/21/3183

Pereau, J. C., Mouysset, L. and Doyen, L. (2016). Groundwater management in food security context. Cahiers du GREThA 2016 14, Université de Bordeaux. A theoretical aquifer model in which a water agency must satisfy an aggregate production minimum while farmers trade permits. The floor is imposed and studied for viability over time, but it is never priced. Useful as proof that imposing a floor and pricing a floor are different acts, and only the second is rare. Link: https://cahiersdugretha.u-bordeaux.fr/2016/2016-14.pdf

Peter, B. G., Siettou, C. and Ramsden, S. J. (2024). Mathematical Programming Model for Multi Objective Smallholder Farming Systems in North East Nigeria. European Journal of Agriculture and Food Sciences, 6 (6), pages 69 to 74. A farm scale linear program in which adding a minimum household food requirement for maize cuts profit by 7 percent, and for maize plus beans by 21 percent. An explicit pricing of a food floor as a share of income, at farm scale only, in a rainfed system with no water allocation. Link: https://eu-opensci.org/index.php/ejfood/article/view/20868

### A7. What Egyptian institutions actually use

Sayed, M. A. A. and Saad, M. B. (2002). The role of Nile Forecast System in managing high floods in Egypt. In Flood Defence 2002, Science Press, New York. Both authors are Ministry of Water Resources and Irrigation officials. The Nile Forecasting System gives decision makers timely forecasts of Nile inflows to the High Aswan Dam, an operational Decision Model sets the dam operating strategy, and the Forecasting System and the Decision Support System are both used in issuing operational products. Analytical decision support at ministry level is documented fact, not a gap. Link: https://iahr.oss-accelerate.aliyuncs.com/library/Proceedings/CosponsoredEvent/Beijing_2002_FloodDefence/V10302.pdf

Ministry of Water Resources and Irrigation (2005). Integrated Water Resources Management Plan, assisted by World Bank staff and consultants. It presents the National Water Resources Plan as the comprehensive framework and documents earlier decision support work, including a Decision Support System for Water Resources Planning project run from 1998 to 2001 by the National Water Research Center. A policy and reform plan: it documents no day to day allocation algorithm and no pricing. Link: https://faolex.fao.org:443/docs/pdf/egy180730E.pdf

National Water Resources Plan 2017 to 2037, Water Security for All. Four objectives and 61 costed measures. Agricultural measures work through growing less water consuming crops, controlling the area of high water consuming crops, water user associations, canal upgrading, metering and water accounting. The instrument is measures and area control, not prices, and no district water values are published with it. Preparation was supported by the European Union under the Water Sector Reform Programme. Links: https://jcar-water.nl/post/5/ and https://www.eeas.europa.eu/eeas/european-union-supports-egypts-national-water-resources-plan_en

Viala, E. (2008). Implementing IWRM in Egypt: From concept to reality. IWRA World Water Congress, Montpellier. Documents what the USAID funded technical assistance actually built: 27 Integrated Water Management Districts, over 600 Branch Canal Water User Associations, and routines for systematic data collection and decentralized flow monitoring over about 1.2 million acres. Organizational and monitoring practice, not economic allocation. Link: https://www.iwra.org/proceedings/index.php?page=286&eventid=2&abstract_id=797

JCAR programme, Joint Cooperation in Applied Research Programme, Water, Egypt and the Netherlands, running since 2021, with Wageningen University and Research, Deltares and Egyptian partners, and Deltares Impact Report 2025. This programme documents the current modelling stack in and around the Ministry of Water Resources and Irrigation: field scale crop modelling with SWAP and WOFOST, regional to sub Delta assessment adding SIWARE, a quantification framework for scenarios and allocation measures, a policy dashboard, RIBASIM training, and a New Agricultural Sector Model of Egypt, the successor to the sector model in Section A2. Deltares states that it built a new water allocation model in RIBASIM for the Nile Delta with the ministry, and that after training the ministry now uses the RIBASIM model. The documented stack simulates allocations under scenarios. Nothing in these sources shows it optimizing farm margin, pricing water by district, or pricing a staple guarantee. Links: https://jcar-water.nl/post/5/ and https://cms.deltares.nl/assets/common/downloads/Impact-report-2025-Deltares.pdf

Abdellatif, M., Gaafar, I., Van Der Vat, M., Hellegers, P., El Naggar, M., Di Miguel Garcia, M. and Seijger, C. (2025). Impact of irrigation modernization and high Aswan Dam inflow on Nile water system efficiency and water reuse in Egypt. Agricultural Water Management, 316, 109576. Authors include ministry Planning Sector and National Water Research Center staff. A RIBASIM and DELWAQ simulation of the Egypt Nile water balance under regular, high and low dam inflow. Baseline system efficiency is already 75.6 percent because of formal and informal reuse, so canal lining raises it only to 78.5 percent. Simulation only: no prices, no margin optimization, no crop choice. Link: https://doaj.org/article/14cf43ecf7d345408a0ce9cad4472332

## Section B. The closest neighbours

**Gohar, Cashman and El bardisy (2021), building on Gohar and Ward (2010 and 2013).** This is the closest work to the whole project. It optimizes water and land jointly inside Egypt in GAMS, with real river nodes, 22 crops, three seasons, a base year balanced to actual gauge flows, and food security and welfare as explicit themes, reporting net benefits, consumer surplus and total welfare under four policy combinations. A Delta district model with a farm margin objective is a disaggregation and simplification of this design, not a new species. What it did not do defines the gap precisely: inflow is the historical average rather than a set of scenarios with the planting decision taken before the scenario is known, geography is river nodes rather than governorates, food security is tracked as an outcome rather than imposed as a floor whose levels are swept and priced, and no governorate table of water duals is published with it.

**Abdelkader and Elshorbagy (2021), the ACPAR framework.** This is the closest work on the income against food question. It treats gross margin, water demand, import dependence and food self sufficiency as simultaneous objectives for the national cropping pattern, compares every pattern against the historical pattern, and hands policy makers a set of quantified trade offs instead of one optimal answer, with a self sufficiency filter at 85 percent of the historical level. The project differs in one sharp way that is worth stating in the paper: ACPAR shows a set of patterns along a trade off surface, while a priced guarantee constraint turns the same trade off into a single number, the margin cost of one more unit of guaranteed staple production, which a decision maker can set beside the cost of importing that unit. ACPAR has no Delta district resolution, no inflow probabilities and no per district water value.

**Adly and co authors (2018).** This is the closest work on the food floor itself, and the natural benchmark for the floor cost. It is the only verified Egypt study that both optimizes a cropping pattern against land and water constraints and computes the income cost of a staple floor: a wheat self sufficiency constraint at 50 percent of consumption that lowers total net revenue by 13.3 percent. A planner reading this project will ask how its floor costs compare, and the paper must have the answer ready. The differences are exact: the Adly floor is one crop, one fixed level, at national scale, inside a deterministic model driven by climate scenarios, with no water prices. This project varies the floor level, prices each level, works at Delta governorate scale, and carries inflow uncertainty in the model structure.

**The Agricultural Sector Model of Egypt lineage: Lofgren (1996), He, Tyner, Doukkali and Siam (2006), McCarl and co authors (2015), continued today as the New Agricultural Sector Model of Egypt inside the ministry modelling stack.** This is the closest work on method credibility and on water prices. It is Egypt own sector model tradition: it derives water shadow prices by varying the water constraint, it tests the taxes and charges a ministry could actually use, it is documented inside Egyptian institutions over three decades, and its data discipline, CAPMAS and ministry cost, price and water use series, is the standard this project data must meet. It differs from this project in working at national scale in economic surplus rather than district farm margin, in being deterministic, and in carrying no staple floor whose price is reported.

**El Shazly and co authors (2009), with Rosa and Yates (1994) behind it.** This is the closest work on stochastic method for Egyptian cropping. El Shazly runs a named two stage stochastic programming model on the Egyptian cropping pattern, with monthly water constraints and food security among its purposes, thirty years after Rosa and Yates first brought stochastic programming to the Egyptian sector model and framed production and trade as stage decisions. Any sentence in the paper that smells of method novelty dies against these two citations. What they did not do is the only room left: neither works at Delta governorate level, neither sweeps and prices a staple floor, and neither reports water duals by governorate and inflow scenario.

## Section C. Positioning: what this project may claim, and what it must never claim

Each candidate claim below was hunted by the skeptic against the verified record. Verdicts use three words only. Proven means the record supports the claim as written. Narrowed means the claim survives only in the exact smaller wording given. Killed means the claim is false against the record and must not appear in any form.

**Claim A. First stochastic programming model of Nile Delta water allocation. Verdict: narrowed, and the word first must be dropped entirely.**
Stochastic programming reached Egyptian cropping in 1994 (Rosa and Yates), a named two stage stochastic model ran on the Egyptian cropping pattern in 2009 (El Shazly and co authors), and stochastic basin allocation including Egypt dates to 2010 (Goor and co authors). What none of the verified work does is restrict the model to the Delta alone, at governorate level, with inflow as the named uncertainty. Even that narrow version asserts a negative over a large Arabic and English grey literature that no search can exhaust, so it should be carried as a scope statement inside the contribution claim below, never as a first claim. Safe wording: a two stage stochastic linear program built only for the Nile Delta at governorate level, with three Nile inflow scenarios.

**Claim B. First model to price a minimum staple food guarantee. Verdict: narrowed.**
Pricing a constraint by comparing income with and without it, or at levels of it, is established Egypt practice. Gohar and Ward (2010) hold food security constraints and report the income comparison; Hamada (2011) states the income cost of a food security respecting reallocation directly; Adly and co authors (2018) compute a 13.3 percent revenue cost for a wheat self sufficiency floor. What the record does not contain is a model that varies a staple production floor at set shares of current staple output and tabulates the margin cost at each level, in a stochastic Delta setting. Safe wording: the model reports total farm margin at staple production floors of 90, 95 and 100 percent of current staple output, so the margin cost of each floor level is shown as a price list for the guarantee.

**Claim C. First district level shadow prices of irrigation water for the Nile Delta, reported by inflow scenario. Verdict: narrowed. This is the strongest claim of the set, and it still does not survive with the word first in it.**
Water shadow prices for Egypt are published at national level (Yates and Strzepek 1998, by scenario; He and co authors 2006, by water availability, in money per cubic metre; Fuglie and co authors 2021, as historical shadow values), and crop level water values exist inside one Delta governorate by the residual method (Saleh 2018, Dakahlia). What the searches did not find is a published set of water duals for each of several Delta governorates, split by low, expected and high inflow, from one optimization model. Safe wording: the model reports a shadow price of water for each governorate in the study area under each of three inflow scenarios, a governorate by scenario set that was not found in the published work searched.

**Claim D. First Egypt model to benchmark the optimized plan against the actual current cropping pattern, and to report that the current pattern fails the full food guarantee in a dry year. Verdict: narrowed. The first half is dead; the second half is a finding, not a novelty claim.**
Benchmarking an optimum against the observed current pattern is the standard design of this literature (Ali and Mahmoud 1999; Dawoud 2014; Hamada 2011; Abdelwahab and co authors 2024). No prior published statement was found that the observed current Delta pattern is itself infeasible against a floor of 100 percent of current staple output under a low inflow scenario. That sentence is this project own model result. It comes with a warning that must travel with it everywhere: project prices, costs, water requirements and district yields are partly assumed today, and an infeasibility result is exactly the kind of result assumed inputs can manufacture. Until real district data replace the assumed data, it is a property of the model, not an established fact about the Delta.

**Claim E. Novelty of the two stage structure itself, planting decided before inflow is known and water allocated after. Verdict: killed.**
Two stage stochastic programming for water resources is textbook material since at least Huang and Loucks (2000), it was applied to Egyptian cropping by El Shazly and co authors (2009), and stage framing appears in Rosa and Yates (1994). Nothing survives. The paper must write instead: the model uses the standard two stage stochastic programming structure, in which planting is decided before the inflow scenario is known and water allocation adjusts after.

**Claim F. Water allocation in the Delta is still done by habit, with no analytical decision support. Verdict: killed.**
The Nile Forecasting System and its Decision Model have supported High Aswan Dam operation for decades (Sayed and Saad 2002). The ministry modelling stack today includes RIBASIM allocation models built for the Delta, SIWARE distribution and reuse models in use since 1991, governorate economic water productivity maps, and remote sensing performance tools. Alongside them sits a shelf of published optimization models. As written, the claim is false. What may be said instead: published optimization models for the Delta exist alongside operational forecasting and decision support systems at ministry level; how far either reaches day to day allocation decisions below ministry level is an open empirical question. A narrower claim about practice at farm or canal outlet level is a claim about behaviour. A modelling paper cannot carry it, and this project must not try.

**The strongest honest contribution claim that survived the skeptic, in full:**
Among the published work searched, no model was found that combines all of the following in one place: a two stage stochastic linear program restricted to the Nile Delta at governorate level; three Nile inflow scenarios with the planting decision taken before the scenario is known; a staple production floor set at 90, 95 and 100 percent of current staple output with the farm margin cost of each level reported, so the food guarantee carries a price list; and a shadow price of water reported for each governorate under each inflow scenario, benchmarked throughout against the observed current cropping pattern. The contribution is the combination, at this scale, for this decision. It is not a new method, not the first stochastic model of Egyptian water, not the first price on Egyptian water, and not the first cost put on a food floor.

**What the paper must never claim, in one list:**
1. First, only or new, for the method, for stochastic programming in Egypt, for water shadow prices in Egypt, or for costing a food security constraint in Egypt.
2. That Delta allocation today runs on habit without analytical support.
3. That the current cropping pattern fails the food guarantee in a dry year, stated as a fact about Egypt rather than as a result of this model on partly assumed data.
4. Any district ranking of water values as a finding, while district yields remain assumed: on current data the ranking restates the assumptions, and only the levels carry information.
5. Any money figure as a measured saving. On current data every pound figure is provisional; ratios and orders are the results, and that grading must stay in the text after the real data land, in whatever form the rerun supports.

## Section D. Lessons worth borrowing

**Price the trade off as a single number, and put the set of patterns beside it.** Abdelkader and Elshorbagy hand policy makers a set of non dominated patterns, which is honest and hard to act on. Adly and co authors hand them one number, a 13.3 percent revenue cost for one floor, which is actable and incomplete. The strong form, and the one this project design already points at, is both: the price list of the guarantee at three levels as the headline, with the pattern set behind it for the reader who wants the surface. A decision maker compares the margin cost of one more unit of guaranteed staples with the cost of importing that unit. Write that comparison into the paper explicitly.

**Report duals, never averages, and say so.** Yates and Strzepek define the water shadow price as a marginal value and show it moving from zero to positive as water binds. He and co authors derive it by varying the water constraint. Fuglie and co authors warn that average water productivity overstates the marginal value of water significantly. The lesson for presentation: every water value in the paper should be a dual from the model, labelled with its scenario, and the paper should state once, plainly, that average return per cubic metre is not a price and is not reported as one.

**Build the baseline the way Gohar builds it.** The Gohar models are balanced to actual gauge flows and observed diversions for a base year, and the Egypt LP tradition from Ali and Mahmoud onward benchmarks against the observed pattern. The World Bank and IPTRID assessment shows why this matters: planned rice area and planted rice area have differed by a factor of two. A baseline built from plans would be fiction. The planned CAPMAS pull serves exactly this lesson: district land, pattern and yields as observed, so the baseline test binds district by district.

**Respect reuse in the water accounting.** El Agha and co authors measured 53 percent of main canal supply returning to drains and groundwater in a Delta command, and Abdellatif and co authors put baseline system efficiency at 75.6 percent for the same reason. A Delta model that treats every cubic metre saved at field level as a cubic metre saved for the system will overstate its own gains. The paper should state how return flows are treated, and discount field level savings accordingly, before a ministry reader does it for the author.

**Present water values the way the ministry already reads them.** The National Water Research Center tool produces governorate maps of economic value of irrigation water, and indicative cropping patterns in ministry strategy already reason in revenue per unit of water. A governorate map of scenario water values, with the guarantee price list beside it, speaks the native format of the intended reader. Tables alone would waste the one presentation asset this project has that the national models lack, which is geography a planner can point at.

**Let the farmers in the paper discipline the objective.** Ghazouani and co authors show Delta farmers planting thirsty crops at canal tail ends for reasons of household food security, know how and neighbour practice, not profit alone. That evidence does two jobs. It justifies the guarantee as something real households already impose on themselves, so the floor is not an analyst invention. And it warns the paper to present the optimized pattern as a direction and a price signal, not as an instruction farmers will follow, because the model objective is only one of the motives in the field.

**Take scenario probabilities from the record, and say where they came from.** Basheer and co authors run Egyptian economic analysis on stochastic hydrologic sequences rather than three hand set states. This project keeps three named inflow scenarios, which is right for a decision maker paper, but the probability of each scenario should be grounded in the long inflow record at Aswan, with the source and the counting rule stated in the data appendix, so the expected values in the paper rest on hydrology and not on round numbers.

**State the food floor in consumption words as well as tonnage.** Adly and co authors define their floor as 50 percent of wheat consumption, a self sufficiency share a minister can repeat. This project floor is set as a share of current staple output, which is precise and abstract. Give both readings in the paper: the tonnage floor, and what it means as a share of the staple supply Egyptians actually eat, so the guarantee reads as food policy and not only as a constraint.
