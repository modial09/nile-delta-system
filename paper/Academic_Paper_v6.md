# Pricing the Wheat Guarantee in the Nile Delta: A Stochastic Allocation Study in Wheat Cover Units

Mohamed Diallo
November 2026

## Executive summary

Egypt consumes about 20.6 million tonnes of wheat a year (U.S. Department of Agriculture, Foreign Agricultural Service [USDA FAS], 2026) and imports a large share of it. The Nile Delta grows much of the home share, on land and water that also carry rice, maize, sugar beet, potatoes and berseem, the clover that feeds Delta livestock. This paper prices a promise: a commitment that the Delta will grow a stated share of national wheat consumption even when water is short. It prices the promise level by level, in the units the decision is taken in: wheat cover, the share of consumption grown at home, and months of stock, the same quantity counted in months of consumption.

The instrument is a two stage stochastic programme over eight Delta governorates, three planting seasons and nine crops. Winter planting is chosen before the year reveals itself; summer and Nili planting, the Nili being a short third season in late summer, adapt to the water state once it is observed. Water states are built the way the river is now run: a starting storage state in Lake Nasser crossed with an inflow year type, turned into a budget by one stated release rule, and each is crossed with a layer, one of three price and yield bands. The guarantee is a hard floor on wheat tonnage that must hold in every scenario at once. Prices are 2025 forecasts from the FAOSTAT farm gate series (Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations [FAO], 2025). Costs stand on the same year through a sourced bridge: an official wheat budget for 2024/25 and the consumer price path for the remaining crops (Agricultural Research Center, 2025; World Bank, 2025). Water scales from a built calibration anchor of 11.00 billion cubic metres that no source publishes, split by land share, and that construction is priced rather than hidden.

The headline result is a price list under planting caps that keep plans near the observed pattern. A guarantee of 15 percent wheat cover, 1.8 months of stock, costs 11.5 billion pounds of expected farm margin; the step to 20 percent cover adds 8.0 billion, and the step to 25 percent cover, 3.0 months of stock, adds 9.4 billion, for totals of 19.5 and 28.9 billion pounds. A guarantee of 30 percent cannot be held at all: the capped ceiling is 27.8 percent cover. Priced on the worst quarter of scenarios rather than the average, the 25 percent promise costs 31.5 billion pounds. The pattern Egypt actually plants already holds 20 percent cover in every scenario, so the operative question is the price of one step, from 20 to 25 percent. In a relaxed world without caps, the same 25 percent promise costs 83.1 billion pounds against a free optimum that plants potatoes at up to 22 times their observed areas. The gap between the lists is itself a finding: a guarantee has no price, only prices, each with its world attached.

Four tests discipline the list. Cutting the water anchor by a fifth raises every capped rung price, to 34.5 billion pounds at 25 percent cover, and does not move the ceiling, because land and caps bind before water. Robustness over twelve scenario weight vectors changes the price by at most 0.04 billion pounds, because a floor that binds in every scenario leaves weights little to move. Replayed against the actual prices and yields of 2015 to 2024, the capped 25 percent plan holds its floor in two years of ten: the promise is a property of the scenario set, and recent history is less kind to it. Wheat yield moves the price most: yields about 11.4 percent above the published basis would halve the cost of the guarantee. Inside the model, recourse sees the full scenario, including its yield layer, before it allocates water, so every printed price is a lower bound. An independent verification passes fifteen of sixteen checks, including replays under a second solver; the single failure is recorded in full in the project verification file. The study is an exploratory model study on a stated basis, not validated empirical evidence, and the final section names the evidence that would change that label.

## 1. Introduction

Food security arguments in Egypt often turn on a single ratio: how much of the wheat the country eats is grown at home. The ratio moves with harvests, imports and stocks, and it is usually quoted after the fact. This paper moves the ratio before the fact. It treats wheat cover as a choice variable with a price, and asks what each level of cover costs the farms of the Nile Delta in expected margin, under uncertainty about water, prices and yields, when the promise must hold in bad years as well as in average ones.

The question matters because the instruments are usually priced apart. A procurement price is set per tonne. A planting plan is drawn per season. A water release is decided per year. None of these prices the combined promise: that a stated share of national consumption will be grown in the Delta whatever the year brings. The contribution here is to price that combined promise directly, level by level and step by step, benchmarked against the cropping pattern Egypt actually plants, and restated in the units of the decision, wheat cover and months of stock. The method is standard. The price list, on this basis, with these checks, is the product.

## 2. Prior work and the narrow contribution

Egyptian water allocation has a long modelling tradition. Sector models have traced the cost of managing with less water at national scale, and shadow prices for irrigation water have been derived at that scale. Gohar, Cashman, and El bardisy (2021) built the fullest Egypt allocation model in the open record, with 22 crops, three seasons and welfare tracked. Stochastic programming reached Egyptian cropping early, and the income cost of a wheat self sufficiency floor has been computed at national scale at one fixed level. Field studies discipline the reading of any optimised pattern: Ghazouani and coauthors (2014) show that Delta farmers plant thirsty crops at canal tail ends for household food security and know how, not for profit alone; El Agha, Molden, and Ghanem (2011) measured about half of main canal supply returning to drains and groundwater in a Delta command, so field level savings are not system level savings.

What that record leaves open is the combination a Delta planner faces in one place: a governorate level model of the Delta alone; planting decided before the inflow is known; a wheat floor swept level by level with the cost of each level and of each step reported; the whole list benchmarked on the observed pattern; and the promise denominated in cover and months rather than in mixed tonnes of unlike crops. Earlier versions of this project priced a floor on aggregate tonnes of wheat, rice and maize. That unit is retired here, because a tonne of feed maize is not a tonne of bread wheat in any decision Egypt actually takes.

## 3. Data and basis

Land, areas and district yields come from the Agricultural Statistics Bulletins of the Ministry of Agriculture and Land Reclamation (2022, 2025), read first hand: physical cultivated area of 1,676,580 hectares across the eight governorates, annual cropped area of 3,176,474 hectares, and published district yields for 2020 to 2021. Land binds by season against physical area, minus a reserve for crops outside the model built from their observed areas. A measured alternative split of that reserve is reported with the results: it moves the price at 20 percent cover by 0.2 billion pounds and does not move the ceiling.

Prices come from the FAOSTAT producer price series, December 2025 vintage, observed to 2024 for wheat, maize and potatoes and to 2023 for rice and sugar beet, carried to 2025 by a forecasting module that races five candidate models per series on rolling origin backtests (FAO, 2025). Simple drift lines won every price series. The low and high layers are quartile bands of the backtest residuals, and their measured coverage is weak, 0.0 to 22.2 percent against a construction value of 50 percent. That weakness is inherited by every expected money figure in this paper. It does not touch feasibility, ceiling or step results, which bind scenario by scenario.

Costs were the hardest data problem of the project, because the official cost bulletin ends in 2022 (Ministry of Agriculture and Land Reclamation, 2022). This version closes the gap with a bridge rather than a bracket: wheat carries the Agricultural Research Center budget for 2024/25, 34,468 pounds per feddan including rent (Agricultural Research Center, 2025), and every other crop carries its 2022 cost moved to 2025 by the consumer price index of Egypt, a factor of 1.959093 (World Bank, 2025). One cell remains a named gap: no winter potato cost table exists, so winter potatoes carry the summer potato cost on the same bridge.

Two crops enter the model the way the Egyptian state and the Delta farm carry them. Rice is bounded in every run by the state decree total of 451,164 hectares (Ministry of Water Resources and Irrigation, 2024; Organisation for Economic Co operation and Development [OECD], 2026); the observed pattern plants 447,153 hectares, inside the bound. Berseem enters as a winter crop in its own right, on ministry figures of 1,253,666 feddans and a fresh yield of 28.637 tonnes per feddan for 2024 to 2025 (Ministry of Agriculture and Land Reclamation, 2025). Its study area total of 359,311 hectares is a stated construction from the national area and the Delta share of 2021, and its price is a stated feed value construction of 0.125 times the maize price, because no official berseem price series exists. Both constructions are bounded by sensitivity in Section 6.

Water is the least measured input. No published series gives release or delivery by Delta governorate. The model therefore scales district budgets from a built anchor of 11.00 billion cubic metres, split by physical land share, and treats the anchor as a priced assumption: every ladder is also solved at 0.90 and 0.80 of the anchor. Section 8 describes the mechanism by which a decision maker holding real district records can replace the split.

## 4. The model

The model is a two stage stochastic linear programme. In the first stage, one winter planting plan is chosen for all scenarios. In the second stage, after a water state is observed, summer and Nili planting adapt to it, and recourse acts: water is reallocated among planted crops, and a planted crop may be abandoned at zero water and zero output with its cost sunk. A kept crop must receive at least 0.30 of its full water duty, with yield falling linearly below full watering; that share is a stated assumption carried from earlier versions. Planting never conditions on the price and yield layer, which is why the wheat floor can be imposed as a hard constraint in every scenario: the plan cannot wait to see prices before it plants.

A scenario is a water state crossed with a layer. A water state is a starting storage state (low, normal, high) crossed with an inflow year type (dry, normal, high). The inflow ratios of the year types, 0.7807, 0.9547 and 1.2213, are taken from the gauged Aswan record of 1871 to 1970, which now serves only to define the types; its frequencies are not used as probabilities, because the High Dam ended the natural river those frequencies describe. The release rule turns a state into a budget: the release factor equals the inflow ratio plus a storage adjustment of minus 0.04 for low storage, plus 0.02 for normal and plus 0.10 for high, bounded between 0.70 and 1.05 of the anchor. Scenario weights are stated planning weights: storage in equal thirds, inflow types at 0.30, 0.45 and 0.25, layers at 0.25, 0.50 and 0.25. Multi year drought enters as named stress budgets rather than as probabilities: a second dry year at the budget of the low storage and dry inflow state, and a third dry year at the release rule floor.

Two planting worlds are priced. In the capped world, each crop is bounded by a stated share of district physical land, carried from the audited earlier version: wheat 0.50, rice 0.35, maize 0.40 in each season, sugar beet 0.30, potatoes 0.15, berseem 0.30. The caps are a modelling device, not a market estimate, and the text says so wherever the capped list is quoted. In the relaxed world the caps are removed. A relaxation of this kind is printed as a benchmark: it shows what the promise costs against a free optimum, and its potato areas, up to 22 times the observed areas at fixed prices, mark the boundary of what fixed prices can credibly describe. The objective is expected farm margin. Beside it, every rung reports conditional value at risk, the mean margin over the worst quarter of scenarios, so the same promise can be priced on the bad tail. A distributionally robust variant replaces the single weight vector with the worst of twelve stated vectors. All runs were solved with CBC through PuLP, and the headline rung and ceiling were replayed under HiGHS by an independent check routine.

## 5. Results

**The capped ladder.** All money figures are billions of pounds of expected margin, rounded to one decimal; exact figures are in the project repair log.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 percent | 0.0 | 221.7 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.0 |
| 15 percent | 1.8 | 210.2 | 11.5 | 11.5 | 13.5 |
| 20 percent | 2.4 | 202.2 | 19.5 | 8.0 | 22.1 |
| 25 percent | 3.0 | 192.8 | 28.9 | 9.4 | 31.5 |
| 30 percent | 3.6 | infeasible | | | |
| 35 percent | 4.2 | infeasible | | | |

The capped ceiling is 27.8 percent cover, 5.73 million tonnes held in every scenario. Three features of the list deserve emphasis. First, the steps are uneven and cheaper in the middle: the step from 15 to 20 percent cover costs less than the step from nothing to 15, because the first promise already moves the plan most of the way. Second, the tail column is close to the average column, within 2.6 billion pounds at the headline rung, so the price is not an artefact of averaging over good years. Third, the capped headline plan plants no rice at all: at the 25 percent wheat floor under caps, rice is crowded out within its decree bound, a result the Pakistan mirror in Section 7 helps to read.

**The relaxed ladder and the pattern.** Without caps, no promise earns 397.3 billion pounds; 15 percent cover costs 34.6 billion; 20 percent costs 55.9 billion; 25 percent costs 83.1 billion; 30 percent costs 116.4 billion; 35 percent costs 149.7 billion; and the ceiling is 42.1 percent cover. The benchmark pattern is more informative than either ladder alone. The observed pattern grows 4.62 million tonnes of wheat on the published basis, a cover of 22.4 percent. Imposed on the model, it holds the 15 and 20 percent levels in every scenario and fails at 25 percent. Under profit seeking recourse it delivers less than it can hold, a worst scenario delivery of 17.4 percent cover. Capacity and delivery are different promises, and only the first is cheap.

**Water, variants and the reserve.** At an anchor of 9.90 billion cubic metres the capped costs at 15, 20 and 25 percent cover rise to 13.4, 21.7 and 31.1 billion pounds; at 8.80 billion they rise to 16.8, 25.3 and 34.5 billion. The capped ceiling does not move at either anchor, and the dry sequence stresses leave relaxed wheat capacity at 42.1 percent after a second dry year and 41.9 percent after a third. Scarcity therefore prices the ladder before it shortens it, on this basis, because a kept crop may run at 0.30 of its full duty. Two variants bound the price from the value side. At the documented wheat procurement price of 14,666.67 pounds per tonne in every layer, the relaxed plan is unchanged and the cost at 25 percent cover rises to 100.9 billion pounds, because the wheat the floor forces is worth less. At all costs 25 percent above the bridge, the relaxed cost at 20 percent cover falls to 49.0 billion pounds, because the free plan earns less. The guarantee cost is an opportunity cost, and both variants move it the way that reading predicts.

## 6. Four tests

**Weights.** Under the distributionally robust objective, the price of robustness is at most 0.04 billion pounds of expected margin at any tested rung, and the worst case gain is 0.03 billion at one rung and nil at the others. Under a common replay, the robust plan holds a marginally lower worst case than the nominal plan at 20 and 25 percent cover, by 0.02 billion pounds; the independent verification records this as a failed ordering check, and no dominance claim is made. The defensible conclusion is narrow: on this ambiguity set, weights are a minor input, because the floor binds in every scenario.

**Tail.** The bad tail prices the headline promise at 31.5 billion pounds against 28.9 billion on the average, a difference of 2.6 billion. A budget holder who distrusts averages loses little by budgeting from the tail at these rungs.

**History.** The capped plan at 25 percent cover was replayed against whole years drawn from the price and yield history of 2015 to 2024, with water at the reference state. Across 200 draws it holds its floor in 18.5 percent of years, with a median capacity of 24.2 percent cover. On the ten actual years in order it holds in 2017 and 2021 only. The observed pattern holds the 25 percent level in none of the ten years. This test is the strongest discipline in the paper: the scenario set is kinder to the promise than the recent decade was, and any use of the price list should carry that sentence with it.

**Weakest input.** Perturbing one input at a time at the capped 25 percent rung, wheat yield dominates: 10 percent below the published basis raises the cost to 45.0 billion pounds, and 10 percent above it cuts the cost to 16.4 billion. The break even point is a yield rise of about 11.4 percent, at which the price of the guarantee halves. Wheat price follows with a swing of 9.4 billion pounds, wheat cost with 6.2 billion, maize price with 4.5 billion, the constructed berseem price with 4.0 billion, potato prices with 3.3 billion, and the water anchor with 2.2 billion. Rice price does not move the headline price, because the headline plan plants no rice. The policy reading is direct: on this evidence, wheat agronomy is a cheaper guarantee instrument than water reallocation.

## 7. A mirror in Pakistan

Punjab offers a matched second budget system and an opposite policy experiment, and it is used here for those two purposes only. Matched crop budgets with rent counted on both sides put Egypt wheat at 239.41 dollars per tonne for 2024/25 against Punjab wheat at 280.67 dollars per tonne for 2023/24, and Egypt rice at 247.18 dollars per tonne for summer 2022 against Punjab rice at 191.98 dollars per tonne for 2022/23 (Agricultural Research Center, 2025; Crop Reporting Service, Punjab, 2024). Egypt grows the promise crop about one fifth cheaper per tonne and the competing crop about one quarter dearer, which is consistent with the model result that a wheat floor under caps crowds rice out. On water productivity, Delta wheat returns 1.05 kilograms per cubic metre of depleted water (El Agha et al., 2011), inside the Punjab range of 0.76 to 1.37 by site and method, so the yield basis that dominates the break even test is not an outlier against the peer system. On policy, Egypt raised its wheat procurement price to 2,500 pounds per ardeb for the 2026 harvest, while Pakistan set no support price for 2024/25 and paid wheat farmers a direct 5,000 rupees per acre instead. The mirror prices no Egypt input. It bounds the cost and yield assumptions, it shows the counterfactual for the procurement lever, and there it stops. Where the mirror is silent, the paper says so: Pakistan publishes fodder only as one combined category, so the constructed berseem price has no foreign cross check and stands as a stated assumption bounded by the sensitivity of Section 6.

## 8. Decision maker data input

The largest data gap in this study, district water by source, belongs to the institutions that would use the price list. Version 6 therefore ships a blank input template with the model: one row per cell a decision maker may hold better numbers for, covering district water supply from canals, drainage reuse and groundwater, crop costs per hectare and crop expected prices per tonne, each labelled in plain English with its unit and its public default. The reader sits at the data loading stage only. A blank cell keeps its default; a filled cell replaces only its named input; and the run log prints the provenance of every cell. Verification confirms that the blank template reproduces the default parameters exactly and that one filled cell changes only that cell. The feature does not close the data gap. It makes the gap fillable by its owner, and any filled run auditable cell by cell.

## 9. Limitations

Four limits govern every figure in this paper. First, district water is built, not measured: the anchor is a calibration and the split follows land, and governorate level readings inherit the construction until real delivery records replace it. Second, the price and yield layers carry measured coverage far below their construction value, so expected money figures are thinner than feasibility, ceiling and step results, which do not depend on the weights. Third, recourse sees the full scenario, including its yield layer, before allocating water; no real season sees that much, so printed guarantee costs are lower bounds. Fourth, two constructions could not be verified against any source: the berseem feed value price and the berseem yield response factor of 1.00. Both are bounded by sensitivity and neither is hidden. The verification record is mixed in one place by design of the check, not by selection: fifteen of sixteen independent checks pass, and the failing robust ordering is reported in Section 6 and in full in the project verification file. What would change the label of this study from exploratory to empirical is named plainly: measured district water by source, a rebuilt layer set with coverage at its construction value, and specialist review of the agronomy. This paper is offered for that review.
