# My Nile Delta Project: The Price Question

Mohamed Diallo, November 2026

## The new question

Version 6 of this project priced a promise about planting. It said what it costs to hold a set share of the wheat Egypt eats growing in the Delta, in good years and in bad: a ceiling of 27.83 percent cover, and 28.9 billion pounds for a 25 percent promise.

But the state does not collect wheat with a planting plan. Farmers grow the wheat, and farmers choose where to sell it. They can deliver to the state at the announced price. They can sell to traders. They can keep it at home or feed it to animals. The announcement the state makes each autumn, the procurement price per ardeb, is the lever that moves that choice.

Version 7 prices the announcement. For each price the state could set, it answers three questions. How much wheat arrives at the collection points? What cover and how many months of stock does that buy? And what does the whole programme cost, gap imports and carrying counted?

The takeaway is early and plain. At the price the state has announced for the coming season, 2,650 pounds per ardeb, the model buys 4.357 million tonnes of deliveries, a cover of 21.15 percent, and misses the state own target of 5.0 million tonnes. In 2,000 simulated seasons at that price, not one reaches the target. Even the top of the public demand range, 3,500 pounds, misses the target on the central reading, by 99,226 tonnes.

## What I added, in three parts

First, the delivery answer. I took the public record of eight seasons, 2019 to 2026. For each season I know the announced price, the tonnes the state bought and the tonnes grown. The share that reached the state is the bought tonnes divided by the grown tonnes. It runs from 0.373 in 2019 to 0.480 in 2026.

Second, the price grid. A price means little alone. It means something against the other price in the farmer hand: the price a trader or the open market pays for the same tonne. So the model works with a ratio, the state price divided by the farm gate price. On six seasons, 2019 to 2024, I fitted one line: the share equals 0.2611 plus 0.1682 times the ratio. In plain words, when the state price rises from equal to the farm price to ten percent above it, the share that arrives rises by about 1.7 points. The line fits those six seasons well. It explains 0.866 of the movement in the shares.

Third, the money layer. At each price the model adds three costs. The bill: the price times the tonnes delivered. The gap cost: the tonnes still missing from the 5.0 million tonne target, imported at a world price. The carrying cost: the interest and the storage on the stock the state now holds. The sum is the total programme cost, the number a minister should see before he signs, not after.

Read the announced row. At 2,650 pounds per ardeb the share is 0.427. On a crop of 10.2 million tonnes, that delivers 4.357 million tonnes. The cover is 21.15 percent of what Egypt eats, 2.54 months of stock. The bill is 77.0 billion pounds. The gap to the target is 642,721 tonnes, and importing it at the central world price of 313 dollars per tonne costs 11.1 billion. Carrying the stock costs 9.3 billion. The total is 97.4 billion pounds.

## The step cost, the number that surprised me

Look at the last column. Each extra tonne the higher price attracts costs between 47,156 and 63,350 pounds, roughly three times the price itself. The reason is simple arithmetic. A higher price is paid on every tonne delivered, not only on the extra tonnes it brings in. The saving on the smaller import gap pays back only a small part. So moving from 2,650 to 3,000 buys 223,792 extra tonnes and costs 12.4 billion extra pounds, about 55,254 pounds for each extra tonne. Most of the money goes to farmers who would have delivered anyway. That may be a fine policy. It should be chosen knowing its price.

## The planting side answers back

Version 6 still runs underneath. In the model, wheat earns a blended price: the delivered share earns the state price, the rest earns the farm gate price. I asked when money alone would plant wheat in the Delta, with no promise and no pattern holding it there. The answer is a state price of about 3,060 pounds per ardeb. Below that point, every hectare of wheat in the model is there because of the pattern Egypt plants and motives outside margin. Above it, the price starts to plant wheat by itself, though slowly: even at 3,500 the free plan plants 116,250 hectares against the pattern of about 672,606 hectares, because potatoes and berseem still take the land and water first.

The same price also makes the old promise cheaper. Holding the pattern production of wheat costs the farm economy 30.2 billion pounds at a price of 2,200, and 13.1 billion at 3,500. A higher price is a more expensive purchase and a cheaper guarantee at the same time.

## Wheat against berseem, an honest negative

The syndicate argument says wheat must beat berseem, its winter rival, before farmers will plant and deliver more. I tested it governorate by governorate. On the model numbers, wheat would need a state price of 5,412 to 6,261 pounds per ardeb to match berseem on margin. No price anyone is asking for comes close. Even if every tonne earned the state price, the break even sits at 4,321 to 5,022.

I print this with its caveat attached. The berseem price in the model is a built feed value, not a market price, because no office publishes one. Berseem mostly feeds the farmer own animals, so its margin is largely feed cost avoided, not cash. Wheat straw and flour for the house carry value the model does not price. So the negative result does not say the price does not matter. It says the delivery answer I fitted is not a berseem margin story. Farmers deliver more at a higher price through faster payment, through obligation, through access to inputs, and because the state price pulls the trader price up with it. The fitted line summarises all of that. The margin table cannot see it. That is why version 7 fits the answer to the record instead of building it from margins.

## The tests I ran

I simulated 2,000 seasons at each price, mixing the three response curves, the production history of 2015 to 2024, and the world price scenarios. At 2,650 pounds, no draw reaches the 5.0 million tonne target. At 3,000, 6.4 percent of draws reach it. At 3,500, 44.5 percent reach it, and the worst quarter there costs on average 138.1 billion pounds against a central total of 129.4 billion.

I moved one new input at a time. The input that moves the total cost most is one I had to assume: how long the state holds the stock. Moving the holding period from three to seven months swings the total at the announced price by 7.5 billion pounds. The world price is second, at 4.8 billion. The response curve itself moves deliveries by about 205,000 tonnes between its low and high forms, but moves the total by only about 0.5 billion, because an extra local tonne replaces an import tonne at similar money. Anyone quoting my 97.4 billion should quote the holding period assumption beside it. I do.

An independent check replayed the work by other paths, including a second solver. Thirteen of thirteen checks pass. One passes weakly, and I keep the weakness visible: the check that the plans respect the rice decree is weak, because the optimised plans plant no rice at all, so the decree bound never binds them. The decree binds the real pattern, not these plans.

## The weak points, plainly

The delivery line rests on six seasons. That is the thinnest evidence in the whole project. Worse, the line already failed a fair test: fitted on 2019 to 2024, it underpredicts the two seasons it did not see. For 2025 it says 0.399 against an actual 0.430. For 2026 it says 0.418 against an actual 0.480. The real answer ran stronger than the old record, in part because planted area rose and payment sped up. My grid holds the crop fixed at 10.2 million tonnes, so it cannot see the planting half of the answer. That is why the grid carries three curves, low, central and high, and why the high curve, fitted on all eight seasons, passes the target at 3,500 while the central curve does not. The honest sentence is this: the target question is a question about which curve the recent record supports, and the recent record runs above my central line.

The grid stretches the line. The fitted ratios run from 0.654 to 1.000. Every price from 2,800 up sits above that range, so those rows are the line extended, not the line observed. The holding period and the storage cost are labelled assumptions with no public source. District water is split by land share from a built total, as in version 6. Recourse sees the full scenario, so plan margins stay low estimates.

## What this part proves

Version 6 priced a promise about land. Version 7 prices the announcement that moves the crop. It hands the price meeting one page: what each price buys, what it costs in full, what it costs in a bad year, and where the answer is soft. The label does not change. This is a numerically verified exploratory study, not validated evidence. The numbers are the start of a price talk, not the end of one.
