What a Wheat Price Buys: Delivery, Money and the Price of a Promise in the Nile Delta

Mohamed Diallo November 2026

Executive summary

Egypt consumes about 20.6 million tonnes of wheat a year and grows roughly half of it at home (USDA FAS, 2026; FAO, 2026). Each autumn the state announces the price it will pay farmers for the coming harvest, and each spring farmers decide how much reaches state collection points and how much goes to traders, to the house, or to animals. For the coming season the announced price is 2,650 pounds per ardeb of 150 kilograms, against public demands of 3,000 to 3,500 (Newsroom.info, 2026; Filcatalog, 2026).

The work stands on a foundation: a two stage stochastic programme in eight Nile Delta governorates, which prices a wheat cover guarantee, a promise that the Delta grows a stated share of national consumption in every water state. Under planting caps the ceiling of that promise is 27.83 percent cover, and a 25 percent guarantee costs 28.9 billion pounds of expected margin, or 31.5 billion priced on the worst quarter of scenarios. The paper then adds the layer the guarantee cannot see. A delivery response, fitted on six of the eight seasons of public record, says how much of the crop each candidate price brings to the state: share equals 0.2611 plus 0.1682 times the ratio of the state price to the farm gate price, R squared 0.866. A financial layer counts each price in full: the bill, the import cost of the shortfall against the target of 5.0 million tonnes, and the carrying cost.

Three results stand out. At the announced price the state buys 4.357 million tonnes on the central curve, cover of 21.15 percent, at a total programme cost of 97.4 billion pounds, and misses the target by 642,721 tonnes; in 2,000 Monte Carlo draws, no draw reaches it. Each extra tonne a higher price attracts costs 47,156 to 63,350 pounds, roughly three times the price itself, because the raise is paid on every tonne delivered. And the margin contest does not explain the response: wheat would need 5,412 to 6,261 pounds per ardeb to beat berseem, far above the demand range, on a berseem price that is itself a stated construction; the response runs through payment speed, obligation and the pull of the state price on the trader price. The fitted line underpredicts both seasons it never saw, so three curves are carried throughout, and the target question becomes which curve the record supports. Independent checks pass thirteen of thirteen for the extension and fifteen of sixteen for the foundation, with failures and weak passes recorded. The study is a numerically verified exploratory study, not validated empirical evidence.

1. Introduction

In October 2026 the Prime Minister of Egypt announced that the state would pay 2,650 pounds per ardeb for the coming wheat harvest (Newsroom.info, 2026). The announcement took minutes. The argument around it had been running for weeks and runs still. Members of parliament and the farmers syndicate asked for 3,000 to 3,500 pounds (Filcatalog, 2026). A deputy on the agriculture committee put the cost of growing one feddan of wheat at about 50,000 pounds for the new season; at an average yield of 18 ardebs and full delivery at the announced price, that feddan returns about 47,700 pounds, and formal briefing requests asked three ministers to review the price (Erem Business, 2026). Read those two numbers together and the stakes stop being abstract. On the figures argued in public, a farmer who delivers his whole crop to the state loses money on every feddan.

What none of the argument prices is the announcement itself. Nobody at the table can say, for a given number, how much wheat it will bring to the state, what the wheat it fails to bring will cost when it is bought later at the port, or what the whole programme costs in a bad year. The price is signed in autumn. The delivery answer arrives the following summer, and the import bill arrives after that. This paper builds the missing page. It treats the procurement price as a decision variable, estimates how deliveries have answered the price in the public record, and attaches a financial layer that counts the bill, the gap and the carrying cost as one number, before the announcement rather than after it.

The paper rests on a companion study with a different promise at its centre. That study asked what it costs the farms of the Nile Delta to guarantee a stated share of national wheat consumption, grown at home, in good water years and in bad ones. Its answer was a price list for the guarantee, level by level, in the units the decision is taken in: wheat cover and months of stock. But a grown tonne is not a delivered tonne. Between the field and the state silo stands a choice the farmer makes at harvest, with the state price in one hand and the trader price in the other. The guarantee model cannot see that choice. This paper can, and joining the two is the contribution: one table in which a price, a delivery forecast, an import gap and a stock carrying cost are priced together, with the uncertainty of the behavioural layer printed beside every row.

The methods are standard, and each is present for a stated reason that the text gives where the method appears. A stochastic programme, because planting is decided before the water year is known. Shadow prices, because a planner needs the price of each constraint and not only a plan. A delivery response fitted to the record, because the margin route to the same answer fails in a way Section 7 demonstrates. Monte Carlo simulation and a ten year backtest, because a fitted promise should be tested against years it never saw. A sensitivity ranking, because a decision maker deserves to know which single fact, if wrong, moves his number most. The product is not the methods. It is the priced table, and the record of checks that lets a reader trust it.

2. The setting, for a reader who does not know Egypt

Three facts carry the whole paper. They are stated here once, with the evidence the project record holds.

The announcement. The procurement price is a cabinet level decision, announced before the winter planting window opens in mid November, so that farmers plant knowing it. For the coming season it is 2,650 pounds per ardeb, up from 2,500 pounds in the 2026 season (Newsroom.info, 2026). The price is argued in cost figures, as the introduction showed, and the argument is a fair description of the decision environment: the number is set by negotiation and pressure, checked against a contested cost estimate and against the import price of the day. There is no agreed model of how much wheat a given price delivers. That absence is the gap this paper works in.

The farmer choice. At harvest the farmer holds a crop the state wants and the market wants. The state buys at more than 400 collection points and has publicised payment within 48 hours (FPC Masr, 2026). The alternatives are traders and private mills, wheat kept for the house, and wheat fed to animals. Parliament members warned in plain terms what a low price does to that choice: farmers cut wheat area, move to crops with faster and higher returns, or sell away from the collection points, and the state imports the difference (Safha News, 2026). The choice is not always free. In 2022 a ministerial decree required every wheat producer to sell a minimum of 12 ardebs per feddan to government purveyors (USDA FAS, 2022), a fact that matters for the estimation in Section 5. The summary statistic of the choice is the delivered share of production, the fraction of the national crop that reaches the state. It is the variable this paper models.

The gap at the port. What the state does not collect, it imports, through the General Authority for Supply Commodities. The record of that buying is reactive and its cost is documented. The tender of August 2024 sought 3.8 million tonnes and bought 280,000 tonnes, about 7 percent of what was sought, with traders citing payment delays of up to 270 days as the reason offer prices rose (Reuters, 2024). Weeks later the authority bought at 275 dollars per tonne, about 40 dollars above a direct purchase price it could not repeat in open tender (Al Manassa, 2024). In June 2022, in the price spike that followed the invasion of Ukraine, it bought 815,000 tonnes at an average of 480 dollars per tonne (USDA FAS, 2022). The pattern across four years is consistent: the state pays a panic premium in bad months and cannot fill volume in cheap ones. One more fact shapes any tool built for this decision. Detailed stock figures are not published continuously; the Minister of Supply told the Senate in October 2026 that disclosure would weaken the state negotiating position with suppliers (Al Hayah News, 2026). A pricing tool for the announcement would live inside the ministry. Its first user is the official who must sign the price, and the standard it has to meet is his: the bad year priced before the signature, not explained after it.

3. Prior work and the narrow contribution

Egyptian water allocation has a long modelling tradition. Gohar, Cashman, and El bardisy (2021) built the fullest Egypt allocation model in the open record, with 22 crops, three seasons and welfare tracked, and shadow prices for irrigation water have been derived at national scale. Stochastic programming reached Egyptian cropping early, and the income cost of a wheat self sufficiency floor has been computed at national scale at one fixed level. Field studies discipline any optimised pattern: Ghazouani and coauthors (2014) show that Delta farmers plant thirsty crops at canal tail ends for household food security and know how, not for profit alone, and El Agha, Molden, and Ghanem (2011) measured about half of main canal supply returning to drains and groundwater in a Delta command, so field level savings are not system level savings.

What that record leaves open is the combination a Delta decision maker faces in one place: a governorate level model of the Delta alone; planting decided before the inflow is known; a wheat floor swept level by level with the cost of each level and each step reported; the promise denominated in cover and months rather than in mixed tonnes of unlike crops; and, new in this paper, the procurement price itself carried as a decision variable, with the delivery answer estimated from the record and the full programme cost attached. Earlier versions of this project priced a floor on aggregate tonnes of wheat, rice and maize. That unit is retired, because a tonne of feed maize is not a tonne of bread wheat in any decision Egypt actually takes.

4. The foundation: pricing a promise about land

Why a stochastic programme. The planting decision and the water year arrive in the wrong order. Winter planting is fixed in November; the inflow that decides the summer water budget is known only later. A model that plants with the year already known would price a promise no farmer and no ministry can actually make. The foundation is therefore a two stage stochastic linear programme over eight Delta governorates, three planting seasons and nine crops, on 1,676,580 hectares of physical land (Ministry of Agriculture and Land Reclamation, 2025). In the first stage one winter planting plan is chosen for all scenarios. In the second stage, once a water state is observed, summer and Nili planting adapt to it, water is reallocated among planted crops, and a planted crop may be abandoned at zero water and zero output with its cost sunk. A kept crop must receive at least 0.30 of its full water duty, with yield falling linearly below full watering. Planting never conditions on the price and yield layer, which is what allows a wheat floor to be imposed as a hard constraint in every scenario at once: the plan cannot wait to see prices before it plants.

Scenarios built the way the river is run. A scenario is a water state crossed with a price and yield layer. A water state is a starting storage state in Lake Nasser (low, normal, high) crossed with an inflow year type (dry, normal, high), giving nine water states and, with three layers, 27 scenarios. The inflow ratios of the year types, 0.7807, 0.9547 and 1.2213, come from the gauged Aswan record of 1871 to 1970, which now serves only to define the types. Its frequencies are not used as probabilities, because the High Dam ended the natural river those frequencies describe; since the dam, water is a release decision buffered by storage. One stated release rule turns a state into a budget: the release factor equals the inflow ratio plus a storage adjustment, minus 0.04 at low storage, plus 0.02 at normal and plus 0.10 at high, bounded between 0.70 and 1.05 of the water anchor. The anchor is 11.00 billion cubic metres, a built calibration that no source publishes, split across governorates by physical land share. That construction is stated wherever the anchor is used, and every ladder is also solved at 0.90 and 0.80 of it, so a reader can watch the price of the assumption. Scenario weights are stated planning weights: storage in equal thirds, inflow types at 0.30, 0.45 and 0.25, layers at 0.25, 0.50 and 0.25. Multi year drought enters as named stress budgets rather than as probabilities: a second dry year at the budget of the low storage and dry inflow state, and a third dry year at the release rule floor.

Why shadow prices. A plan alone tells a decision maker what to plant. It does not tell him what the binding limits cost. The programme is read through its dual values as well as its plan: the shadow price of the water constraint in a governorate is what one more cubic metre earns there at the margin, and the shadow cost of the wheat floor is what the promise itself charges the system. Those prices, not the plan, are what a ministry allocating releases and weighing a guarantee actually trades in. They run through both studies, and the ladder below is read off them: the shadow cost of the wheat floor at each level is the price of that rung.

Two planting worlds. In the capped world each crop is bounded by a stated share of district physical land, carried from the audited earlier version: wheat 0.50, rice 0.35, maize 0.40 in each season, sugar beet 0.30, potatoes 0.15, berseem 0.30. The caps are a modelling device that keeps plans near the observed pattern; they are not a market estimate, and the text says so wherever the capped list is quoted. In the relaxed world the caps are removed, and the list is printed as a benchmark: it shows what the promise costs against a free optimum, and its potato areas, up to 22 times the observed areas at fixed prices, mark the boundary of what fixed prices can credibly describe. Rice is bounded in every run by the state decree total of 451,164 hectares (Ministry of Water Resources and Irrigation, 2024; Organisation for Economic Co operation and Development [OECD], 2026); the observed pattern plants 447,153 hectares, inside the bound. Berseem, the clover that feeds Delta livestock and the largest winter planting at 1,253,666 feddans in 2024 to 2025 (Ministry of Agriculture and Land Reclamation, 2025), competes with wheat for winter land inside the model. Its study area total of 359,311 hectares is a stated construction from the national area and the Delta share of 2021, and its price is a stated feed value construction of 0.125 times the maize price, because no official berseem price series exists. Both constructions return in Section 7, where they are priced rather than merely confessed.

Data and basis. Prices come from the FAOSTAT producer price series, December 2025 vintage, observed to 2024 for wheat, maize and potatoes and to 2023 for rice and sugar beet, carried to 2025 by a forecasting module that races five candidate models per series on rolling origin backtests (FAO, 2025). Simple drift lines won every price series, and the paper reports that rather than dressing the forecast in a method that lost. The low and high layers are quartile bands of the backtest residuals, with measured coverage of 0.0 to 22.2 percent against a construction value of 50 percent. That weakness is inherited by every expected money figure in this paper; it does not touch feasibility, ceiling or step results, which bind scenario by scenario. Costs stand on one year, 2025, through a sourced bridge: wheat carries the Agricultural Research Center budget for 2024/25, 34,468 pounds per feddan including rent (Agricultural Research Center, 2025), and every other crop carries its official 2022 cost moved to 2025 by the consumer price index of Egypt, a factor of 1.959093 (World Bank, 2025). One cell remains a named gap: no winter potato cost table exists, so winter potatoes carry the summer potato cost on the same bridge.

The ladder. Under caps, the guarantee is priced level by level. A promise of 15 percent cover, 1.8 months of stock, costs 11.5 billion pounds of expected farm margin. The step to 20 percent adds 8.0 billion, for a total of 19.5 billion, and the step to 25 percent, 3.0 months of stock, adds 9.4 billion, for a total of 28.9 billion. A promise of 30 percent cannot be held at all: the capped ceiling is 27.83 percent cover, 5,733,795 tonnes held in every scenario. The steps are uneven and cheaper in the middle, because the first promise already moves the plan most of the way. The pattern Egypt actually plants grows 4,617,795 tonnes on the published basis, a cover of 22.42 percent; imposed on the model it holds the 15 and 20 percent levels in every scenario and fails at 25 percent, and under profit seeking recourse it delivers less than it can hold, a worst scenario delivery of 17.40 percent cover. Capacity and delivery are different promises, and only the first is cheap. In the relaxed world the same 25 percent promise costs 83.1 billion pounds. The gap between the lists is itself a finding: a guarantee has no single price, only prices, each with its world attached.

Why the list is tested four ways. Each test answers a distinct doubt, and each is reported with its result, including the one that fails. First, the scenario weights are judgment, not measurement, so a plan that rests on them is suspect. A distributionally robust variant therefore replaces the single weight vector with the worst of twelve stated vectors. Its yield is a near null result, and it is informative: robustness changes the price by at most 0.04 billion pounds, because a floor that binds in every scenario leaves the weights little to move. One part of the test fails, and Section 8 records it in full: under a common replay the robust plan holds a marginally lower worst case than the nominal plan at 20 and 25 percent cover, by about 0.02 billion pounds, so no dominance claim for the robust plan is made anywhere in this work. Second, an average year is not the year that breaks a budget, so every rung also reports conditional value at risk, the mean margin over the worst quarter of scenarios. Priced on the bad tail, the 25 percent promise costs 31.5 billion pounds against 28.9 billion on the average, a tail premium of 2.6 billion. Third, a fitted promise should be tested against years it never saw. The capped plan at 25 percent cover, replayed on 200 whole years drawn from the price and yield history of 2015 to 2024, holds its floor in 18.5 percent of draws; on the ten actual years in order it holds in 2017 and 2021 only, and the observed pattern holds the 25 percent level in none of the ten. History is less kind to the promise than the scenario set is, and any use of the price list should carry that sentence with it. Fourth, a decision maker needs to know which single fact, if wrong, changes his answer. Perturbing one input at a time at the headline rung, wheat yield dominates: 10 percent below the published basis raises the cost to 45.0 billion pounds, 10 percent above it cuts the cost to 16.4 billion, and a yield rise of about 11.4 percent would halve the price of the guarantee. Wheat price follows with a swing of 9.4 billion pounds, then wheat cost at 6.2 billion, maize price at 4.5 billion, the constructed berseem price at 4.0 billion, potato prices at 3.3 billion, and the water anchor at 2.2 billion. Rice price does not move the headline price at all, because the headline plan plants no rice. The policy reading is direct: on this evidence, wheat agronomy is a cheaper guarantee instrument than water reallocation.

5. The delivery response: the farmer answer, estimated from the record

Why the record, and why a ratio. The extension needs one behavioural input the foundation did not: when the state raises the announced price, how much more of the wheat grown actually arrives at state collection points? Two routes were open. The first derives the answer from margins, crop against crop. Section 7 shows where that road ends: on the model numbers, wheat cannot outbid berseem at any price under discussion, so a margin derived response would report that the price does nothing, which the record plainly contradicts. The second route reads the answer off the record of what farmers actually did. This paper takes the second route, with its weaknesses in the open.

The record is eight seasons, 2019 to 2026, assembled from USDA FAS Cairo reporting, FAO GIEWS briefs, ministry announcements and Reuters, giving for each season the announced price, the tonnage the state bought, national production, and the delivered share (FAO, 2026; USDA FAS, 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023, 2025).

The price variable is a ratio: the procurement price per tonne divided by the farm gate price of wheat per tonne in the same season, both from the FAOSTAT producer price family (FAO, 2025). The ratio is the right shape for the farmer decision, because the state price never competes with silence. It competes with the price the trader offers for the same tonne, at the same gate, on the same day. Two warnings travel with the ratio and neither is hidden. In 2022 and 2023 the published farm gate price equals the procurement price exactly, 5,900 and 10,000 pounds per tonne, so the ratio reads 1.000 in those years by construction of the sources rather than by market observation: the official alternative series is partly administered. And part of the 2022 share was required by the delivery decree of Section 2, not chosen at the price. The fit that follows is a summary of how share and ratio moved together in a short, policy heavy record. It is not a clean market experiment, and this paper does not present it as one.

The fit. Ordinary least squares of share on ratio, on the six seasons with a published farm gate price, gives share equals 0.2611 plus 0.1682 times ratio. The slope carries a standard error of 0.0331 and the intercept a standard error of 0.0286; the residual standard deviation is 0.0105 of share; the line accounts for 0.866 of the variance of the six shares. In words: when the state price rises from equal to the farm gate price to ten percent above it, the fitted share rises by about 1.7 points, from about 0.429 to about 0.446. Refitted without 2022, the season with the legal requirement, the line is share equals 0.2831 plus 0.1384 times ratio; the slope falls by about a fifth. The 2022 season stays in the headline fit because the requirement bound only a minimum per feddan and the season still carries price information, but the refit is reported here and the difference is part of the uncertainty the three curves below have to carry.

Validation, and the honest failure. No farm gate price is published for 2025 or 2026, so those seasons cannot be fit points. Used as checks, with the alternative price set to the project forecast of 17,892 pounds per tonne, the line underpredicts both: for 2025 it gives 0.399 against an actual share of 0.430, and for 2026 it gives 0.418 against an actual 0.480, misses of about 0.3 and about 0.6 million tonnes. The misses run in one direction, and they are informative rather than merely embarrassing. The response of the two recent seasons ran stronger than the earlier record, and the public record says why in part: planted area rose to a record of about 3.7 million feddans in 2026, payment within 48 hours was publicised, and the price for 2026 was announced before sowing and raised again in April 2026. A fit that holds production fixed cannot see the planting half of the response. The extension handles this in three stated ways. The headline grid holds production fixed and says so. Three curves are carried through every result: a low curve, share equals 0.2893 plus 0.1351 times ratio, the fit with its slope reduced by one standard error; the central curve above; and a high curve fitted on all eight seasons, share equals 0.2464 plus 0.1989 times ratio, resting on a constructed alternative price for the last two seasons and labelled as a construction wherever it is used. And the Monte Carlo of Section 7 varies production across its historical range on top of the curves, pricing the uncertainty the grid itself cannot model.

6. The price grid and the financial layer

Why a tonne is not a cost. A delivery forecast alone would still leave the decision maker with arithmetic to do at the worst moment. The financial layer exists because the state does not buy tonnes; it pays a bill, imports a gap and carries a stock, and the three move in opposite directions as the price rises. At each candidate price the programme cost has three parts. The bill is the price times the tonnes delivered. The gap is the announced delivery target of 5.0 million tonnes, the goal the government set for the 2026 season (Reuters, 2026), less the tonnes delivered, imported at a world price scenario: 265, 313 or 400 dollars per tonne cost and freight, each anchored on a named dated observation, the central value being the S&P Global price for wheat delivered to Egypt on 8 October 2026 (S&P Global, 2026), converted at 54.47, 55.25 or 57.00 pounds per dollar (Central Bank of Egypt, 2026; Reuters, 2026). Gap orders are priced at the arrival quarter scenario, carrying a stated shipping lag of 75 days, a labelled assumption. The carrying cost is charged on delivered local tonnes only: financing on the bill at treasury bill yields of 0.23956 to 0.2635, central 0.25, over a holding period of three, five or seven months, plus storage at 40, 60 or 90 pounds per tonne per month. The holding period and the storage cost are labelled assumptions; no public source prices either, and Section 7 shows what that costs the headline. The total counts these three parts only. It excludes the baseline import programme for the rest of consumption, about 10.4 million tonnes on the basis used here, which does not change across the grid, and the bread subsidy system downstream of the stock.

Read the announced row in full. At 2,650 pounds per ardeb the bill is 77.0 billion pounds, the gap of 642,721 tonnes costs 11.1 billion to import at the central world price, carrying costs 9.3 billion, and the total is 97.4 billion. The state buys 4.357 million tonnes against a target of 5.0 million, cover of 21.15 percent, 2.54 months of stock.

The step column carries the grid its sharpest lesson, and the lesson is arithmetic, not estimation. Each extra tonne a higher price attracts costs 47,156 to 63,350 pounds, roughly three times the procurement price itself. The reason is that the higher price is paid on every tonne delivered, not only on the extra tonnes it attracts, and the import saving on the shrinking gap offsets only a small part of that. Moving from the announced 2,650 to the bottom of the demand range at 3,000 buys 223,792 extra tonnes for 12,365,166,213 extra pounds, an average of 55,254 pounds per extra tonne. Most of that money goes to farmers who would have delivered at the lower price. That may be good policy; income support to wheat farmers is a legitimate aim. What the grid adds is the price tag, so the aim can be chosen rather than stumbled into.

The top of the demand range does not settle the target question, and this paper refuses to pretend otherwise. At 3,500 the central curve delivers 4.901 million tonnes, still 99,226 tonnes short of the target, at a total of 129.4 billion pounds. On the high curve the same price delivers 5,159,464 tonnes and passes the target. The target question is therefore a question about which response curve the recent record supports, and the recent record, the two seasons the central fit never saw, runs above the central line. A smaller reading at the bottom of the grid deserves one sentence because it is easy to misread: at 2,200 the total is almost flat across the three curves, 83.2 billion on the central curve and a little less on the low and high, because at a low price an extra delivered tonne swaps an import tonne at the world price for a local tonne at a similar all in cost. Higher up the grid the bill dominates and the total rises with the curve; at 3,500 the band runs from 128.1 billion pounds on the low curve to 134.5 billion on the high.

The planting side answers back. The grid also rereads the cropping model at each price, with wheat revenue valued at a blended price: the delivered share at the state price, the rest at the farm gate price of the same layer. Asked when margin alone would plant wheat in the Delta, with no floor and no pattern imposed, the model answers at a state price of about 3,060 pounds per ardeb, the point where the blended price reaches about 19,027 pounds per tonne. That point sits inside the demand range. Below it, on the model basis, every hectare of wheat stands on the observed pattern and on motives outside margin. Above it the price begins to plant wheat by itself, slowly: even at 3,500 the free plan plants 116,250 hectares against the pattern area of about 672,606 hectares, because potatoes and berseem still take the land and water first. The cost of holding the pattern production floor falls as the price rises, from 30.2 billion pounds at 2,200 to 13.1 billion at 3,500. A higher state price is therefore also a cheaper guarantee: the same production promise costs the farm economy less to keep when wheat is worth more at the collection point. The two studies meet in that sentence. The guarantee model priced the promise as if the crop arrived by itself; the price model shows the announcement quietly paying part of the guarantee bill.

7. Robustness of the extension

Monte Carlo, and what it can and cannot say. Two thousand draws per price combine the three response curves at weights of 0.25, 0.50 and 0.25, a production multiplier drawn from whole years of the 2015 to 2024 record, an alternative price drawn from the three foundation price layers, and a world price scenario drawn from the three scenarios. The design mirrors the foundation backtest in spirit: a central case is a promise about a constructed world, and the draws test how the promise behaves when the world is sampled from the record instead.

The target reading is the hard one. At the announced price, no draw in 2,000 reaches 5.0 million tonnes. At 3,000, 6.4 percent of draws reach it. Only at 3,500 does reaching the target become a realistic prospect, at 44.5 percent of draws, and the worst quarter mean cost there is 138.1 billion pounds against a central case total of 129.4 billion. A second reading, the share of draws reaching the promised cover at each price, sits near one half at every price by construction, because the promise is the central case and the draws are centred near it. It is reported in the project record for completeness and should not be quoted as a confidence level; this paper mentions it only to quarantine it.

Break even against berseem, an honest negative. The public argument assumes farmers deliver more when wheat beats berseem on margin. The model can price that contest exactly, and the answer kills the story. On the model basis berseem earns a margin of 129,327 pounds per hectare, on a price of 2,566.62 pounds per tonne built from the constructed feed value, a yield of 68.183 tonnes per hectare and a cost of 45,674 pounds per hectare. Wheat must therefore earn revenue of 211,394 pounds per hectare to match it, against a wheat cost of 82,067 pounds per hectare. The break even state price at the blended value, where only the delivered share earns the state price, runs from 5,412 pounds per ardeb in Sharkia, the highest yielding governorate at 7.338 tonnes per hectare, to 6,261 in Kafr El Sheikh, the lowest at 6.314. At full delivery, the most favourable case for wheat, it runs from 4,321 to 5,022. No price in the grid and no price in the demand range makes wheat beat berseem on margin in any governorate. The caveats are part of the result and travel with it: the berseem price is a constructed feed value, not a market price; berseem in the Delta feeds the farmer own animals, so its margin is largely avoided feed cost rather than cash; wheat straw and household flour carry value the model does not price; and berseem area cannot grow past livestock demand, which the model cap of 30 percent of district land reflects. The honest reading is that the delivery response is not a berseem margin story. Farmers deliver more at a higher price through payment speed, obligation, input access and the state price anchoring the trader price, channels the fitted share summarises and the margin comparison cannot see. That is why this paper fits the response to the record instead of deriving it from margins, and why the negative result strengthens rather than embarrasses the design: the two routes were both walked, and the paper reports where each one ends.

Sensitivity, and the warning it carries. Each new input moved between its low and high values at the announced price, all else central, ranked by the swing in total programme cost: the holding period, a labelled assumption with no public source, swings the total by 7.5 billion pounds; the world price by 4.8 billion; the production basis by 1.8 billion, with deliveries swinging by 712,817 tonnes; storage cost by 1.1 billion; the financing rate by 0.8 billion; the response curve by 0.5 billion, with deliveries swinging by 204,831 tonnes; and the exchange rate by 0.5 billion. The ranking is the most uncomfortable table in the paper, and it is printed in full for that reason. At the announced price the total is more sensitive to an assumption nobody can source than to any measured input, and more sensitive to the world price than to the delivery response itself, because extra local tonnes replace import tonnes at similar money. Anyone quoting the total at 2,650 should quote the holding period assumption beside it. This paper does, in the executive summary numbers as in this section: the 97.4 billion is a five month holding number.

A mirror in Pakistan. Punjab offers a matched second budget system and an opposite policy experiment, and it is used for those two purposes only. Matched crop budgets with rent counted on both sides put Egypt wheat at 239.41 dollars per tonne for 2024/25 against Punjab wheat at 280.67 dollars per tonne for 2023/24, and Egypt rice at 247.18 dollars per tonne for summer 2022 against Punjab rice at 191.98 dollars per tonne for 2022/23 (Agricultural Research Center, 2025; Crop Reporting Service, Punjab, 2024). Egypt grows the promise crop about one fifth cheaper per tonne and the competing crop about one quarter dearer, consistent with the foundation result that a wheat floor under caps crowds rice out. On policy, Egypt raised its price to 2,500 pounds per ardeb for the 2026 harvest while Pakistan set no support price for 2024/25 and paid farmers a direct 5,000 rupees per acre instead: the counterfactual for the price lever, run live by a peer system. The mirror prices no Egypt input; it bounds the cost and yield assumptions, and there it stops. Where it is silent, the paper says so: Pakistan publishes fodder only as one combined category, so the constructed berseem price has no foreign cross check.

8. Verification and research practice

The numbers in this paper are only worth reading because of the procedure that produced them, and the procedure is part of the work, so it is described here as research practice rather than as an afterthought.

Independent checks, written apart from the build. Each version carries a check routine that recomputes the headline numbers by paths the build did not use. For the foundation, src/check_v6.py passes fifteen of sixteen checks, including replays of the headline rung and the ceiling under HiGHS, a second solver, agreeing with the build solver to within about a million pounds on objectives of about 200 billion. The sixteenth check fails: the distributionally robust ordering check of Section 4. The failure is recorded in full in the project verification file, with the size of the discrepancy, and no dominance claim for the robust plan appears anywhere in this work. For the extension, src/check_v7.py passes thirteen of thirteen checks: the response fit recomputed by a second method to the printed digits, its R squared recomputed at 0.865938, grid arithmetic at three prices recomputed from the saved inputs, the two announced price plans replayed under HiGHS with agreement at solver tolerance, the Monte Carlo shares recomputed from the saved sample file including the worst quarter mean total of 100,533,845,324 pounds exactly, and one break even price recomputed directly from the parameters. One of the thirteen passes weakly and is recorded as weak: the check that the saved floor plan respects the rice decree passes with the whole bound to spare, because the optimised plans plant no rice at all. At the model prices rice earns the least per unit of water among the summer crops, so margin seeking plans drop it; the decree binds the observed pattern, which plants 447,153 hectares against a bound of 451,164, and does not bind these plans. The check therefore tests almost nothing for the extension, and the verification file says so in those words. A check that cannot fail is not evidence, and the record treats it that way.

Claim discipline. Every headline claim in the project papers is entered in a claim register with its inputs, its generating file and a status: confirmed computation, reported, or unverified. The register has teeth. In an earlier version it caught a printed claim that the historical pattern holds no guarantee at any level, a claim the saved outputs contradicted; the claim was corrected in the text and the correction is part of the record. The register discipline is why the failures in this paper are findable: the robust ordering failure, the weak rice pass, the underprediction of 2025 and 2026, and the weak measured coverage of the price layers are all stated in the documents that carry the numbers, not in a separate confession.

The repository and the version path. The work lives in a public record of its own revisions: the repository github.com/modial09/nile-delta-system, held private while the work is under review, with the code, data files, run logs and documents versioned together. The path matters to the argument. Version 5 rebuilt the foundation and corrected the price vintage and the baseline claim. Version 6 repaired the four foundation faults a blind persona review had named: the promise restated in wheat cover and months of stock instead of mixed tonnes; the water anchor narrated as built, with district supply turned into a working input; the scenarios rebuilt on storage, inflow year types and a stated release rule instead of the frequencies of a river that no longer runs naturally; and rice bounded by the decree with berseem standing inside the model as a real winter crop. Version 7 added the delivery response, the price grid and the financial layer. Each version was checked before the next was built on it, and the run records and results files regenerate from the code, which the independent verifications confirmed by replay.

The decision maker input template. The largest data gap in the study, district water by source, belongs to the institutions that would use the price list. The model therefore ships a blank input template at data/external/decision_maker_input_template.csv: 42 rows covering district water supply from canals, drainage reuse and groundwater, crop costs per hectare and crop expected prices per tonne, each row labelled in plain English with its unit and its public default. The reader sits at the data loading stage only, in src/user_template_v6.py, called from one place inside the loading routine; no solver code and no model structure were changed for the feature. A blank cell keeps its public default; a filled cell replaces only its named input; and the run log prints the provenance of every cell, user or default. Verification confirms that the blank template reproduces the default parameters exactly and that one filled cell changes only that cell. The feature does not close the data gap. It makes the gap fillable by its owner, and any filled run auditable cell by cell. That distinction, between a model that hides its gaps and a model that hands them to the person who can fill them, is the working standard of the whole project.

9. Limitations

Six limits govern every figure in this paper. First, the behavioural layer rests on six fit seasons from a policy heavy record, in a market where the state is both price setter and largest buyer, one season carried a legal delivery requirement, and the alternative price series is partly administered. The fitted slope is a planning input with wide honest uncertainty, not a measured elasticity of a free market. Second, the headline grid holds production fixed at 10.2 million tonnes, while the two validation seasons suggest production itself answers the price through planted area; the grid is therefore conservative on deliveries in a way the paper can state but cannot yet correct, and a two equation version, area and share together, is the obvious next build and the obvious next place to be wrong. Third, every grid row at 2,800 pounds and above extrapolates the response beyond its observed ratio range of 0.654 to 1.000. The demand range lives in the extrapolated region, which is uncomfortable and is stated rather than smoothed over. Fourth, two financial inputs, the holding period and the storage cost, are labelled assumptions with no public source, and the first of them is the largest single sensitivity of the headline total. Fifth, the foundation limits stand: district water split by land share from a built anchor of 11.00 billion cubic metres, so governorate level readings inherit a construction; price and yield layers with measured coverage of 0.0 to 22.2 percent against a construction value of 50 percent, so expected money figures are thinner than feasibility, ceiling and step results; and recourse that sees the full scenario, including its yield layer, before allocating water, so printed plan margins and guarantee costs are lower bound readings. Sixth, the berseem price is a constructed feed value with no market series and no foreign cross check; the break even result of Section 7 is printed with that caveat attached and should not be quoted without it. What would move this study from exploratory toward empirical is named plainly: delivery records by governorate and week, which the state payment system plausibly holds; measured district water by source; a market observed alternative price series; and specialist review of the agronomy. The paper is offered for that review.

10. Conclusion

The official who signs the wheat price each autumn signs it inside an argument conducted in cost figures and pressure, and he learns its consequence the following summer, at the collection points and then at the port. This paper gives him the consequence first. For each price he might sign, it states what the price buys in tonnes and in months of cover, what it costs in full once the gap is imported and the stock is carried, what it costs in the worst quarter of simulated years, and which parts of the answer rest on six seasons of record, which on extrapolation, and which on assumptions nobody can yet source. The headline is uncomfortable in a useful way: the announced price does not buy the announced target on the central reading of the record; raising the price buys its extra tonnes at about three times their face value, because the raise is paid on every tonne; and the strongest recent evidence says farmers answer the announcement more strongly than the older record predicts, through planting and payment channels a share equation cannot see. None of that ends the argument about the right price. It changes what the argument is about, from competing certainties to a priced table with its soft places marked. That is the value the work offers the stakeholder. The label stands as it stood for the foundation: a numerically verified exploratory model study on a stated basis, not validated empirical evidence, offered for review by the people who can supply the data that would change the label.

Appendix A. Formulation

Sets. Districts d in D, the eight Delta governorates: Beheira, Sharkia, Dakahlia, Kafr El Sheikh, Gharbia, Menoufia, Qalyubia and Damietta. Crops c in C, nine in the model: wheat, berseem, sugar beet and winter potatoes in winter; rice, summer maize and summer potatoes in summer; Nili maize and Nili potatoes in the Nili season. Water states w in W, a storage state in {low, normal, high} crossed with an inflow year type in {dry, normal, high}. Layers l in L, {low, expected, high}. Scenarios s = (w, l) in S, 27 in all, with stated weights pi(s): storage in equal thirds, inflow types at 0.30, 0.45 and 0.25, layers at 0.25, 0.50 and 0.25.

Parameters. A(d), physical land of district d, totalling 1,676,580 hectares, binding by season net of a reserve for crops outside the model built from their observed areas. B(w), the water budget of state w: the anchor of 11.00 billion cubic metres times a release factor, the inflow ratio of the year type (dry 0.7807, normal 0.9547, high 1.2213) plus a storage adjustment (low minus 0.04, normal plus 0.02, high plus 0.10), bounded between 0.70 and 1.05, split across districts by physical land share. Duty(c), the full water duty of crop c per hectare. Y(c, d, l), the yield of crop c in district d at full watering under layer l. P(c, l), the price of crop c under layer l. K(c), the cost of crop c per hectare. Cap(c), the planting cap of crop c as a share of district land. R, the rice decree bound of 451,164 hectares. Qcon, national wheat consumption of 20,600,000 tonnes. F, the cover level under test, as a share of Qcon.

Decision variables. First stage: x(d, c), winter hectares of crop c in district d, one plan for all scenarios. Second stage, per water state w: z(d, c, w), summer and Nili hectares; and per scenario s: u(d, c, s) in [0, 1], the share of the full water duty delivered to a planted hectare of crop c, with u = 0 reading as abandonment at sunk cost. Production of crop c in district d under scenario s is Y(c, d, l) times the planted hectares times the yield response of u, linear between the floor share and full watering: a kept crop receives at least 0.30 of its duty, and its yield scales in proportion to the water delivered above that floor.

Objective. Maximise expected farm margin, the sum over scenarios of pi(s) times total revenue minus total cost, revenue being the sum over districts and crops of P(c, l) times production, cost the sum of K(c) times planted hectares in both stages. The guarantee cost at level F is the unconstrained expected margin less the expected margin of the same programme with the wheat floor imposed at F. The tail reading replaces the expectation over scenarios with the conditional value at risk of margin at the worst 25 percent tail. The robust variant replaces the single weight vector pi with the worst case over an ambiguity set of twelve stated weight vectors.

Constraints. Land: for each district and season, planted hectares across the crops of that season do not exceed A(d) net of the reserve. Caps: for each district and crop, planted hectares do not exceed Cap(c) times A(d), in the capped world only. Water: for each district and scenario, the sum over crops of Duty(c) times u(d, c, s) times planted hectares does not exceed the district share of B(w). Rice: for each water state, total rice hectares across districts do not exceed R. Wheat floor: for each scenario s, total wheat production across districts is at least F times Qcon. Non negativity throughout, with u bounded as stated.

The delivery response. Let p be the state price per tonne, q the farm gate alternative price per tonne, and r = p / q the price ratio. Let s(r) be the share of national production delivered to state collection points and Q the production basis, 10,200,000 tonnes in the headline grid. Deliveries are

D = s(r) * Q,    s(r) = a + b * r

fitted by ordinary least squares on the six seasons 2019 to 2024 with a published farm gate price. Central curve: a = 0.2611, b = 0.1682, slope standard error 0.0331, intercept standard error 0.0286, residual standard deviation 0.0105, R squared 0.866, n = 6. Low curve: a = 0.2893, b = 0.1351. High curve, fitted on all eight seasons with a constructed alternative price for 2025 and 2026: a = 0.2464, b = 0.1989. Refit without 2022: a = 0.2831, b = 0.1384. Out of sample: fitted 0.399 against actual 0.430 in 2025; fitted 0.418 against actual 0.480 in 2026. Observed ratio range of the fit: 0.654 to 1.000. Grid ratios run to 1.304 at 3,500 pounds per ardeb; rows above a ratio of 1.000 are extrapolations.

The financial layer. With T the delivery target of 5,000,000 tonnes, w the world price per tonne in pounds at the arrival quarter scenario, f the financing rate, h the holding period in months, and c the storage cost per tonne per month, the total programme cost at price p is

C(p) = p * D  +  w * max(0, T - D)  +  p * D * f * h / 12  +  D * c * h

Central parameters: w at 313 dollars per tonne cost and freight converted at 55.25 pounds per dollar, scenarios at 265 and 400 dollars and exchange rates of 54.47 and 57.00; f at 0.25, range 0.23956 to 0.2635; h at 5 months, range 3 to 7, labelled assumption; c at 60 pounds per tonne per month, range 40 to 90, labelled assumption. Carrying is charged on delivered local tonnes only. One identity drives the grid: between adjacent prices, the step cost per extra delivered tonne is dominated by the inframarginal term, because the price rise is paid on all D tonnes rather than on the increment; numerically the step cost runs from 47,156 to 63,350 pounds per extra tonne against prices per tonne of 14,667 to 23,333.
