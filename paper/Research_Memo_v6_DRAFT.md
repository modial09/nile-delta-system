# Pricing the Wheat Guarantee: A Research Memo on the Nile Delta Model, Version 6

Mohamed Diallo
November 2026

Every result in this memo is a verified model result on the basis stated in Section 1. None of it is a measured fact about Egypt, and no sentence in this memo should be read as one. The full run record, with exact figures, sits in docs/foundation_rebuild/S6_Repair_Log.md. The verification record sits in docs/foundation_rebuild/CheckV6_Independent_Verification.md. Money in this memo is rounded to billions of pounds with one decimal. Exact pounds live in the repair log only.

## 1. The question, the method, the central result

**The question.** Egypt eats about 20.6 million tonnes of wheat a year (U.S. Department of Agriculture, Foreign Agricultural Service [USDA FAS], 2026), and grows a part of it in the Nile Delta, where the same water is divided among eight governorates and the crops they plant. A planner who wants to promise that the home grown share will hold in a dry year has no published price for that promise. I built this model to produce the price list: which wheat promises the study area can support inside the model, what each promise costs in expected farm margin, and under which stated assumptions each price holds.

**The units of the promise.** Version 5 of this work priced a floor on mixed tonnes of wheat, rice and maize added together, as if each tonne were the same food. Version 6 retires that unit. The promise is now stated the way Egypt states it in practice: wheat cover, the share of national wheat consumption grown at home, and months of stock, the same quantity divided by one month of consumption. A cover of 25 percent is a promise of 5.15 million tonnes of wheat, which is 3.0 months of stock.

**The method.** The model is a two stage stochastic programme over eight governorates and nine crops. Three words carry the design, and each is defined where it first appears. The Nili season is a third, short planting season in late summer, after the main summer season; the model plants winter crops, summer crops and Nili crops. A layer is one band of prices and yields: a low band, an expected band and a high band, built from the backtest errors of a forecasting module. Recourse is the set of choices a planner still holds after the year reveals itself: once a scenario is realised, water can be reallocated among planted crops and a planted crop can be abandoned, at zero water and zero output with its cost already spent. Winter planting is one plan for all scenarios, fixed before the year is known. Summer and Nili planting carry one variant per water state, decided with the inflow observed. No planting decision conditions on the layer. The guarantee is a hard floor on wheat tonnage, held in every scenario at once, because a promise that holds only on average fails in the very years it was bought for.

**The scenarios describe a managed river.** Version 5 weighted its inflow states by the frequencies of natural flow at Aswan from 1871 to 1970. That river no longer exists: since the High Dam, the water a season receives is a release decision buffered by Lake Nasser. Version 6 builds each water state from a starting storage state (low, normal, high) crossed with an inflow year type (dry, normal, high), and turns the pair into a budget by one stated release rule: the release factor equals the inflow ratio of the year type plus a storage adjustment, bounded between 0.70 and 1.05 of the anchor. The old record survives only as the definition of the year types. The weights on the 27 scenarios are stated planning weights, not measured frequencies, and Section 5 prices what that choice is worth. Dry sequences are priced as named stresses: a second dry year at the driest budget in the set, and a third dry year at the release rule floor.

**The basis.** Prices are 2025 forecasts from the FAOSTAT farm gate series, observed to 2024 for wheat, maize and potatoes and to 2023 for rice and sugar beet (Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations [FAO], 2025). Costs stand on the same year, 2025, on a sourced bridge: wheat at the Agricultural Research Center budget for 2024/25 of 34,468 pounds per feddan including rent, and every other crop at its official 2022 cost moved to 2025 by the consumer price path of Egypt, a factor of 1.959093 (Agricultural Research Center, 2025; Ministry of Agriculture and Land Reclamation, 2022; World Bank, 2025). Yields are the published district yields of 2020 to 2021. The water budgets scale from a built calibration anchor of 11.00 billion cubic metres that no source publishes, split across governorates by physical land share. The anchor is a construction, and every figure in this memo inherits it. Section 5 prices what a wrong anchor does.

**The central result.** Read the capped price list first, because it prices the promise in the world closer to the one Egypt plants. Under named planting caps, a guarantee of 25 percent wheat cover, 3.0 months of stock, costs 28.9 billion pounds of expected margin against the capped optimum of the same model, and 30 percent cannot be held at all, because the capped ceiling is 27.8 percent. The steps are what a budget holder negotiates: the step from no promise to 15 percent cover costs 11.5 billion pounds, the step to 20 percent adds 8.0 billion, and the step to 25 percent adds 9.4 billion. In the relaxed world with no caps, where planting may leave the observed pattern without limit, the same 25 percent promise costs 83.1 billion pounds and the ladder reaches 35 percent at 149.7 billion, with a ceiling of 42.1 percent. The pattern Egypt actually plants already holds 20 percent cover in every scenario. One warning travels with every price in this memo: inside the model, recourse sees the full scenario, including its yield layer, before it allocates water. No real season sees that much. The printed guarantee costs are therefore lower bounds on the cost of the promise, on this count.

**The implication.** A guarantee level is a budget decision, and it should be taken from a price list with its conditions printed beside it. This memo hands over that list at three water anchors and under two planting worlds, with the current pattern tested on the same basis, with four added methods that test the list against its own weights, its own tail, its own history and its own weakest input, and with every weakness that survives verification named in Section 7.

## 2. The decision, the prior work, and the narrow contribution

The reader this work serves is a planning authority for the Delta: a ministry level planner who sets seasonal planting guidance and divides irrigation water among districts, and who may be asked to promise that the wheat harvest will hold in a dry year. The model does not choose the guarantee level. It publishes what the choice needs: the margin at each level, the cost of each level against the free optimum and against the level below, the highest level that can be held at all, and the same list repriced when the water anchor is wrong.

I am not the first to model Egyptian water, and this work claims no such thing. Egypt has a sector model tradition of three decades, and Gohar, Cashman, and El bardisy (2021) built the fullest Egypt allocation model I found, with 22 crops, three seasons and welfare tracked. Stochastic programming reached Egyptian cropping early, and the cost of a wheat self sufficiency floor at national scale has been computed before at one fixed level. What that record leaves open is the combination a Delta planner faces in one place: a model restricted to the Delta at governorate level, with planting decided before the inflow is known, a wheat floor swept level by level with the margin cost of each level and each step reported, benchmarked against the observed current cropping pattern rather than a planned one, and now restated in the units the decision is actually taken in, wheat cover and months of stock. That combination is the contribution, and it is deliberately narrow.

Two lessons from the field literature discipline every reading. Ghazouani and coauthors (2014) show that Delta farmers plant thirsty crops at canal tail ends for household food security and know how, not for profit alone, so an optimised pattern is a direction and a price signal, not a planting instruction. El Agha, Molden, and Ghanem (2011) measured about half of main canal supply returning to drains and groundwater in a Delta command, so field level savings are not system level savings. My model works on applied duties at district level and claims no saving of that kind.

## 3. Data, timing, and the model

Land, cropping pattern and district yields come from the Agricultural Statistics Bulletins of the Ministry of Agriculture, read first hand: the eight governorate panel for 2020 to 2021, with the potato season split from 2021 to 2022. The model keeps two land quantities apart at all times. Physical cultivated area, 1,676,580 hectares, limits each season. Annual cropped area, 3,176,474 hectares, describes the year and limits nothing, because a field carrying two crops is still one field. Land binds by season against physical area, minus a reserve for the crops I do not model, built from their observed areas. Section 5 prints the measured alternative split of that reserve beside the primary one, in full: the capped ceiling is 27.8 percent under both splits, and at 20 percent cover the winter first split prices the guarantee 0.2 billion pounds higher than the primary split. The reserve choice moves the price a little and the ceiling not at all.

Two crops are carried the way the Egyptian state carries them. Rice, whose area the state fixes by decree each year and which washes salt from the northern Delta, is bounded in every run by the decree total of 451,164 hectares (Ministry of Water Resources and Irrigation, 2024; Organisation for Economic Co operation and Development [OECD], 2026). The observed study area plants 447,153 hectares of rice, inside the bound. Berseem, the clover that feeds Delta livestock and the largest winter planting of all, stood outside the version 5 model entirely. It now stands inside it as a winter crop competing with wheat, on ministry figures: 1,253,666 feddans planted in 2024 to 2025 at a fresh yield of 28.637 tonnes per feddan, with a cost of 9,792 pounds per feddan including rent for 2021 to 2022 (Ministry of Agriculture and Land Reclamation, 2022, 2025). Its study area total of 359,311 hectares is a stated construction from the national area and the Delta share of 2021, because no district berseem areas are published in the sources I hold. Its price is also a construction, a feed value of 0.125 times the maize price, because no official berseem price series exists. Section 5 bounds what that construction can do to the headline: a 10 percent move in the berseem price moves the headline guarantee cost by about 4.0 billion pounds.

Costs taught me the largest lesson of this project, and version 6 closes the gap version 5 could only bracket. The official cost series ends in 2022. Version 5 answered with guessed multipliers. Version 6 answers with a bridge: an official wheat budget for 2024/25 from the Agricultural Research Center, and the consumer price path for everything else, both held in one workbook with their sources named (Agricultural Research Center, 2025; World Bank, 2025). One cost cell remains a named gap: no winter potato cost table exists in the bulletin, so winter potatoes carry the summer potato cost, bridged to 2025 like the rest.

The model does not eat hand set prices or yields. A forecasting module races five candidates per series on rolling origin backtests, and drift lines won every price series in version 5; version 6 keeps that module and its outputs unchanged, including its honest weakness: the low and high layers are quartile bands of the backtest residuals, and their measured coverage is weak, 0.0 to 22.2 percent against a construction value of 50 percent. Feasibility and ceiling results do not depend on the layer weights, because the floor binds scenario by scenario. Expected money figures do, and they inherit the thinness.

Timing is stated exactly, because it decides what the guarantee means. Winter planting is one plan for all 27 scenarios. Summer and Nili planting carry one variant per water state. A relaxation, in the language of this work, is the relaxed ladder of Section 5: the same model with the planting caps removed, printed as a benchmark and never as a recommendation. Its optima plant potatoes at up to 22 times their observed areas at fixed prices, and at those multiples the fixed prices stop being credible, which is the whole reason the capped list leads.

## 4. The decision maker can bring their own numbers

The weakest input in this model is district water. No public source gives canal, reuse and groundwater supply by Delta governorate, and the default split by land share assumes water follows land, which no canal system does. Version 6 therefore treats district supply as a user input, as a working feature and not as a promise. A blank template ships with the model. It has one row per cell a decision maker may hold better numbers for: water supplied to each district from canals, from drainage reuse and from groundwater; the cost of each crop per hectare; and the expected price of each crop per tonne. Each row is labelled in plain English with its unit and its public default. The model reads the template at the data loading stage only. A blank cell keeps its public default. A filled cell replaces only its named input, and the run log prints, cell by cell, which values came from the user and which from the defaults. Two checks stand behind the feature: with the blank template in place, the model reproduces the default budgets, costs and prices exactly; and with one filled cell, only that cell changes. A demonstration in the run record moves two districts by 20 percent in opposite directions: the ceiling does not move, and the price at 20 percent cover moves by 0.1 billion pounds. The feature does not repair the data gap. It hands the gap to the one reader who can repair it, and it makes any use of their numbers auditable.

## 5. The guarantee trade off

**The capped price list is the headline.** All figures are billions of pounds of expected margin, rounded to one decimal. Exact figures are in the repair log.

| Cover | Months of stock | Expected margin | Cost against the capped optimum | Step cost from the rung below | Cost on the bad tail (CVaR) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 percent | 0.0 | 221.7 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.0 |
| 15 percent | 1.8 | 210.2 | 11.5 | 11.5 | 13.5 |
| 20 percent | 2.4 | 202.2 | 19.5 | 8.0 | 22.1 |
| 25 percent | 3.0 | 192.8 | 28.9 | 9.4 | 31.5 |
| 30 percent | 3.6 | infeasible | | | |
| 35 percent | 4.2 | infeasible | | | |

The last column prices the same promise on the bad tail of the scenario set: conditional value at risk, the average margin across the worst quarter of scenarios, charged against the same measure at no promise. At 25 percent cover the tail price is 31.5 billion pounds against an average price of 28.9 billion. A planner who distrusts averages can budget from the tail column, and the difference at the headline rung is 2.6 billion pounds.

**The relaxed ladder is the benchmark.** The same promise, priced where planting may leave the observed pattern without limit: no promise earns 397.3 billion pounds; 15 percent cover costs 34.6 billion; 20 percent costs 55.9 billion, a step of 21.4 billion; 25 percent costs 83.1 billion, a step of 27.2 billion; 30 percent costs 116.4 billion; 35 percent costs 149.7 billion; and the ceiling is 42.1 percent, 5.1 months of stock. The gap between the two lists is the single most useful number in this memo after the headline itself: the price of a guarantee is mostly a property of the world it is priced against, and no honest quotation of it exists without its world attached.

**The current pattern is a stronger base than the public argument assumes.** On the published basis the pattern grows 4.62 million tonnes of wheat, a cover of 22.4 percent. Imposed on the model, it holds the 15 percent and the 20 percent levels in every scenario, and fails at 25 percent. Left to profit under recourse it delivers less than it can hold: its worst scenario delivery is 17.4 percent cover. The live policy question is therefore not the rescue of a failing pattern. It is the price of the step from the 20 percent the pattern already holds to the 25 percent a planner might promise, and under caps that step costs 9.4 billion pounds.

**Water binds on price, not on the ceiling.** At an anchor of 9.90 billion cubic metres the capped costs rise to 13.4, 21.7 and 31.1 billion pounds at 15, 20 and 25 percent cover. At 8.80 billion they rise to 16.8, 25.3 and 34.5 billion. The capped ceiling does not move at either anchor, and the dry sequence stresses barely move wheat capacity in the relaxed world either: a second dry year leaves it at 42.1 percent, and a third dry year at the release rule floor leaves it at 41.9 percent. The reason is structural: at the ceiling, land and the planting caps bind first, because a kept crop may run at 0.30 of its full water duty with a linear yield penalty. Scarcity raises the price of every rung long before it deletes one. That is a different warning from version 5, and a more useful one: a guarantee policy written at the full anchor does not silently expire in a dry decade, it silently gets more expensive.

**Two price variants, briefly.** At the documented wheat procurement price of 14,666.67 pounds per tonne in every layer, the relaxed plan at each tested level is unchanged and the guarantee costs more, because the wheat the floor forces is worth less: 70.1 billion pounds at 20 percent cover and 100.9 billion at 25 percent, against 55.9 and 83.1 billion at forecast prices. At all costs 25 percent above the consumer price bridge, the relaxed cost at 20 percent cover falls to 49.0 billion pounds, because the free plan the promise is priced against earns less. Both variants say the same thing from opposite sides: the guarantee cost is an opportunity cost, and it moves with the value of what the floor displaces.

## 6. Four tests of the price list, and a mirror

**Weights.** The scenario weights are a stance, so the capped ladder was rebuilt under a distributionally robust objective: the plan must do well not under one weight vector but under the worst of twelve, the nominal weights plus stated tilts toward dry states, low storage and the low layer. The result is small in both directions. Robustness costs at most 0.04 billion pounds of expected margin at any tested rung, and buys a worst case gain of 0.03 billion at one rung and nothing at the others; under a common replay the robust plan in fact holds a marginally lower worst case than the nominal plan at 20 and 25 percent cover, by 0.02 billion pounds. The independent verification records this as a failed ordering check, in full, and this memo claims no dominance for the robust plan. The honest reading is that the weights barely matter here, because the floor is hard in every scenario: feasibility never depends on them, and the price moves only through the average.

**Tail.** The CVaR column of the headline table is the second test, and its result is printed with the ladder: the bad tail prices the headline promise 2.6 billion pounds above the average.

**History.** The third test replays the capped plan at 25 percent cover against the actual price and yield history of 2015 to 2024, whole years drawn as they came. In 200 draws the plan holds its floor in 18.5 percent of years; its median capacity is 24.2 percent cover, just under the promise. On the ten actual years in order it holds in two, 2017 and 2021. The historical pattern holds the 25 percent level in none of the ten years. This is the hardest result in the memo to read and the most important one to print: the guarantee is a promise about the constructed scenario set, and the recent history of prices and yields is less kind to the same plan than the scenario set is. A planner who buys the 25 percent promise on this evidence is buying a plan that the last decade would have broken four years in five, unless planting, water or prices move from their recent pattern. The model prices the promise; it does not insure it.

**Weakest input.** The fourth test perturbs one input at a time at the headline rung. Wheat yield moves the guarantee price most by a wide margin: yields 10 percent below the published basis raise the cost of the 25 percent promise to 45.0 billion pounds, and yields 10 percent above it cut the cost to 16.4 billion. A yield rise of about 11.4 percent halves the price of the guarantee, and at double yields the price is zero, because the free plan then plants enough wheat by itself. Wheat price is second, with a swing of 9.4 billion pounds; wheat cost third, at 6.2 billion; then maize price, the constructed berseem price, potato prices and, last, the water anchor itself, at 2.2 billion. Rice price does not move the headline price at all, because the capped headline plan plants no rice. The practical reading: the guarantee is priced mostly by how much wheat a hectare grows, and only second by water. Agronomy is the cheapest guarantee policy this model can see.

**A short mirror in Pakistan.** Punjab runs the same contest on the other side of the policy question, and the mirror is printed here for one reason: it checks the shape of my Egypt assumptions against a second official budget system, and it shows the counterfactual for the procurement lever. Matched budgets, each with rent counted inside, put Egypt wheat at 239.41 dollars per tonne for 2024/25 against Punjab wheat at 280.67 dollars per tonne for 2023/24, and Egypt rice at 247.18 dollars per tonne for summer 2022 against Punjab rice at 191.98 dollars per tonne for 2022/23 (Agricultural Research Center, 2025; Crop Reporting Service, Punjab, 2024; as assembled in the project workbook). Egypt grows wheat about one fifth cheaper per tonne and rice about one quarter dearer. On water, Delta wheat returns 1.05 kilograms per cubic metre of depleted water against a Punjab range of 0.76 to 1.37 by site and method (El Agha et al., 2011). On policy, Egypt raised its wheat procurement price to 2,500 pounds per ardeb for the 2026 harvest while Pakistan set no support price at all for 2024/25 and paid wheat farmers a direct 5,000 rupees per acre instead. The mirror carries no Egypt number into the model. It bounds three assumptions, in the table below, and then it stops.

| Egypt assumption in this model | Matched Pakistan value | What the match bounds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wheat cost of 82,066.67 pounds per hectare on the 2025 bridge, about 239.41 dollars per tonne | Punjab wheat at 280.67 dollars per tonne, 2023/24 | The Egypt wheat cost is not an outlier against a peer budget system; it sits below the Punjab figure |
| Rice cost on the 2022 bulletin, bridged, about 247.18 dollars per tonne | Punjab rice at 191.98 dollars per tonne, 2022/23 | Egypt rice is the expensive crop of the pair, which supports reading the model result that the capped headline plan crowds rice out |
| Berseem priced by construction at 0.125 times the maize price | No Punjab berseem figure exists; Pakistan publishes fodder only as one combined category | The construction cannot be cross checked abroad; it stands as a stated assumption, bounded only by the sensitivity in Section 3 |
| Wheat yield basis from published district yields | Punjab wheat water productivity of 0.76 to 1.37 kilograms per cubic metre against the Delta figure of 1.05 | The Delta yield basis sits inside the peer range; the break even result, that yield moves the guarantee price most, is not an artefact of an extreme yield basis |

## 7. Limitations, and what evidence would change the label

This study is a numerically verified exploratory model study on a stated basis. It is not validated empirical evidence about the Delta, and four gaps block the stronger label. First, district water is built, not measured: the anchor is a calibration and the split follows land, and until delivery or release records by district enter through the template of Section 4, every governorate level figure inherits the construction. Second, the price and yield layers are inherited from the version 5 forecasting run with measured coverage far below their construction value; expected money figures inherit that thinness, while feasibility, ceiling and step results do not depend on the weights. Third, recourse inside the model sees the full scenario before it allocates water, so every printed guarantee cost is a lower bound; a recourse that answers only the water state would raise every price by an amount nobody has measured. Fourth, two carried constructions could not be verified against any source: the berseem feed value price, and the berseem yield response factor of 1.00. Both are bounded by sensitivity in this memo, and neither is hidden.

The verification record deserves one plain paragraph. An independent check routine recomputed the bridges from the workbook itself, recomputed the release rule from the inflow series, replayed the headline capped rung and the capped ceiling under a second solver, and tested the template feature for isolation. Fifteen of sixteen checks pass. The failing check is the robust ordering of Section 6, recorded in full in docs/foundation_rebuild/CheckV6_Independent_Verification.md. Nothing in this memo rests on the failed item.

What would change the label: measured district water by source, from the records of the authority that holds them, entered through the template and repriced; a rebuilt layer set with measured coverage at its construction value; and a review of the agronomy by an Egyptian irrigation or agricultural specialist. Professor review is the next step, and this draft is written for it.
