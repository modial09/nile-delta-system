# Nile Delta Water Allocation Project Version 3 Explainer Script

## Part 1 The problem simply

Imagine you must plant today, but you will only learn later how much river water will arrive. That is the daily reality in the Nile Delta. Supply is fixed by the river and by agreements, demand keeps growing, and a food promise sits in the middle. Farmers plant wheat, rice, maize, sugar beet and potatoes across eight governorates. If the year turns dry, there is not enough water to give every planting its full need. The core question of this project is simple to state and hard to answer. What planting plan protects food best, what does that protection cost, and where is one extra cubic metre of water worth most when water is scarce.

## Part 2 The process part one the failure and the rebuild

This project did not start clean. Versions one and two ran on partly invented numbers. Several yields, prices, water needs and district patterns were assumed rather than sourced. An internal audit replayed the arithmetic, attacked the claims, and killed the headlines that the invented ground could not support. Instead of polishing the story, the work stopped and the ground was rebuilt. Every parameter for version three had to come from a named source with a date, or be labelled openly as a gap, a choice, or a calibration. That discipline is the foundation of everything that follows.

## Part 3 The process part two how the data connects

Six families of data join together, and each one has a clear job. First, prices come from FAOSTAT for Egypt from 1991 to 2023, with the main run using 2023 values. Second, production costs come from CAPMAS bulletins for 2016 to 2017, the latest complete official tables found. Third, land, cropping pattern and yields come from Ministry panels for 2020 to 2021, covering all eight governorates and all five crops. Fourth, applied water requirements tell how much water each crop needs on the field. Fifth, yield response factors from FAO work tell how production falls when water falls short. Sixth, one hundred years of gauged flow at Aswan from 1871 to 1970 are turned into three scenarios with probabilities and budgets. Here is how they connect. Yield times price gives revenue per hectare. Revenue minus cost gives margin per hectare. The real district pattern gives the baseline and also gives district yield multipliers, because each governorate is compared with the production weighted mean for that crop. Water requirement against the scenario budget creates scarcity. Scarcity is what makes the choices real.

## Part 4 How the model computes

The model works in two stages, in the order real decisions happen. Stage one chooses hectares by governorate and crop before the inflow is known. Stage two allocates water after the scenario arrives. A staple food guarantee is added as a rule. Staple production from wheat, rice and maize must reach a set share of the current pattern output in every scenario. That share is swept at 90 percent, then 95 percent, then 100 percent, so the guarantee has a price list rather than a slogan. From the solved model we read dual values. A dual value answers a precise question. If this governorate had one more cubic metre in this scenario, how much would total margin rise. Those duals are checked by brute force, by adding a small amount of water and solving again. After the run, an independent audit replayed every headline figure from the raw logs and rebuilt the feasibility question with a second solver and separate arithmetic.

## Part 5 What came out

The headline is a ceiling, not a gain. Under the main scenario derivation, no plan can hold a 95 percent or a 100 percent staple floor in every scenario. The highest floor any plan can hold is 93.65 percent. The reason is physical. Full watering for the current pattern needs 16.724 billion cubic metres, while the low scenario budget is 8.591 billion cubic metres. The total model budget anchor is 11.00 billion cubic metres, and that figure is a calibration, not a measured official budget for the eight governorates. At the 90 percent floor that can be held, the optimised margin is 47.74 percent above the baseline when sugar beet is priced at 795 pounds per tonne, and 52.92 percent above the baseline when sugar beet is priced at 2400 pounds per tonne. That gain must be spoken with its honesty line in full. Most of the gain growth against the old version comes from the current pattern failing under real water needs, and the current pattern delivers about 69.5 percent of its full water staple output in expectation. Sugar beet shows why price concepts matter. At 795 the plan plants no beet at all. At 2400 it plants about 321075 hectares, receives almost no water in a dry year, and about 5.65 billion pounds of planting cost is sunk. In a dry year, water values range from about 43.91 to 78.57 pounds per cubic metre across governorates and variants. The result is also fragile. When every water budget is cut by 5 percent, the gain at 90 percent falls to 8.15 percent.

## Part 6 Value and use

Public tools already do important parts of this work. The Nile Forecasting System supports ministry decisions on inflow and dam operation, but its governorate water values and priced guarantee outputs are not published for public reuse. RIBASIM models for the Delta are now used by the ministry after training, as simulation tools, and in the public sources checked no priced guarantee list was published with them. SIWARE studies simulate water, crops and salinity in parts of the Delta, and they do not price water. Published academic models, including work by Gohar and coauthors, optimise water and land for Egypt as a whole and track food security, but they do not publish a Delta governorate table that sweeps a staple floor and prices each level. FAO WaPOR tools help the public monitor water productivity from satellites, which is monitoring, not allocation pricing. This project adds a small and specific thing, in the open, with code, data provenance and an audit trail that anyone can replay. Ministry planners can use it to set a food floor they can actually defend, and to see the margin price before they promise a level that physics cannot hold. Governorate water managers can use the dry year map to see where one cubic metre is worth most, and where protection matters first. Farmers and their advisers can use the planting and coverage results to see which crops survive a dry year and which plantings carry the highest risk when a high price tempts them to plant into scarcity. For the author, Mohamed Diallo, the value is reviewable evidence of method. The numbers matter, but the process matters more, because every figure can be traced, challenged and rebuilt.
